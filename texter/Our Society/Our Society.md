Society is large and complicated. It is easy to see the parts, but harder to see how they fit together. This page builds up the picture of our society step by step. Each step adds a new part, and at the end you see the whole Society Map.

You do not need to read everything at once. Each step has links onwards if you want to know more.

## Where are we heading?

Towards a sustainable society: a good life for all, within planetary boundaries. That is the vision, and it is the starting point for everything else on this site.

The vision has two edges. One is a social foundation that no person should have to fall below. The other is an ecological ceiling that no generation may break through. Between them lies the space where a good life is possible for everyone.

The vision says where we are going, but not how we get there. Why it can be shared by everyone, whatever their politics, is described on the page [The Vision](<The Vision/The Vision.md>).

## What sets the limits?

The planet. Everything we do, eating, living, travelling and producing, depends on nature working: a stable climate, clean air and water, fertile soil and a living diversity of species.

Scientists have identified nine processes that together keep the Earth stable. They are called the [planetary boundaries](<../Society Explorer/Framework/Planetary Boundaries/Planetary Boundaries.md>). According to the latest assessment, from 2025, seven of the nine have already been crossed. That does not mean disaster is here, but that we have left the zone where the risks are small.

This is the outermost frame of the map. Everything else in society fits inside it.

## What do people need?

Roughly the same things everywhere: food, water, housing, health, security, relationships, learning and being able to take part. On this site they are called **basic needs**. Together they form the social foundation.

Basic needs are few, but the ways of meeting them are many. That is where sustainability is decided. Research shows that the planet has enough for the foundation for everyone. But no country today manages to meet its people's basic needs in a way that would be sustainable if everyone lived that way. There is more about this on the page [A Good Life](<../Society Explorer/The Social and Cultural System/A Good Life/A Good Life.md>).

That is why [People](<../Society Explorer/People/People.md>) have a page of their own. People are not a system of their own, but are present in all of them. Society exists for people, and it is people who shape it.

## How is society organised?

In four systems that build on each other. To meet our needs, we have built a society with many parts. Here they are divided into four societal systems. None of them is more important than the others, and none manages without the others.

The [ecological system](<../Society Explorer/The Ecological System/The Ecological System.md>) is the foundation. It is the nature we live from and in: the cycles, the resources and the diversity that keeps everything going. Without it, none of the others works.

The [economic system](<../Society Explorer/The Economic System/The Economic System.md>) organises work, production and distribution. It is where nature's resources become food, homes and services, and where most people earn their living.

The [social and cultural system](<../Society Explorer/The Social and Cultural System/The Social and Cultural System.md>) is about relationships, norms, culture and trust. It decides what we mean by a good life and whether we trust each other enough to do things together.

The [democratic system](<../Society Explorer/The Democratic System/The Democratic System.md>) is where we decide together. It sets the rules for the other systems and decides which direction society takes.

## How do the parts fit together?

More closely than it seems. A change in one system almost always has consequences in the others.

Take an example. Today the economy needs to grow for there to be enough jobs. Work gives not only income but also identity and community, so when jobs disappear it becomes a social question. That is why whoever promises jobs and economic growth wins elections. And GDP growth has so far required more and more energy and materials, taken from nature. Four systems, one single chain.

The chain also runs the other way. When nature is harmed, for example by drought or floods, it hits the economy, people's security and trust in those who govern.

Sometimes the chain reinforces itself. Drought makes harvests more expensive, worry grows, and it becomes harder to agree on what to do. This is called a **feedback loop**, and it is why small changes sometimes have large consequences.

That is why a problem can rarely be solved in one system at a time. Those who see only one part risk moving the problem instead of solving it.

## Why is it so hard to change?

Because the faults lie in how the systems work, not in people being bad or stupid. Four patterns recur in all four systems:

- **Short-termism.** Electoral terms, quarterly reports and budget years reward what shows quickly, while the costs come later.

- **Externalisation.** Costs are pushed onto others, onto nature, other countries or future generations, and so do not show in the price.

- **Imbalance of power.** Those who profit from the present order often have more influence than those who pay for it.

- **Silo thinking.** Each agency, industry and ministry is responsible for its own part, but nobody is responsible for the whole.

The patterns reinforce each other. What is profitable in the short term and whose costs can be pushed onto others gets done, and those with power can keep it that way.

## What needs to change?

The direction, more than individual details. Faults built into the systems cannot be corrected with one-off reforms. What is needed is a shift in what the systems prioritise. Five improvements recur throughout the site:

- The whole before the parts.

- The long term before the short term.

- Responsibility before externalisation.

- Participation before concentration of power.

- Regeneration before depletion, meaning that nature is allowed to recover and be rebuilt instead of being drained.

This site does not propose which policies should be pursued to get there. That is the task of democracy, and the parties compete over the path. What is covered is how the direction can become visible and open to scrutiny, so that voters can see where the path leads. That is described on the page [Elections and Parties](<../Society Explorer/The Democratic System/Elections and Parties/Elections and Parties.md>).
