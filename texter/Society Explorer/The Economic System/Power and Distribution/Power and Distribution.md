## Why do some people have much more money than others?

Because differences in income and ownership reinforce each other over time. Income from work varies, but ownership of companies, homes, land and financial assets affects people's economic situation even more.

Those who already own assets get income from them and find it easier to save and invest. That is why wealth is more unevenly distributed than income. In the world as a whole, the richest tenth own around 76 per cent of all wealth, while the poorest half own 2 per cent.

Economic differences are therefore not only about what people earn right now, but also about which resources they have access to and can build on.

## Why does money affect people's opportunities so differently?

Because financial margins give room for action. Those who have margins can more easily handle unexpected expenses, get an education, change jobs, start a business or invest.

Those with small margins instead have to prioritise the bare necessities and have less room to act for the long term.

Differences in resources thus become differences in people's actual opportunities to shape their lives.

## How does ownership affect the development of society?

Ownership gives control over resources and therefore influence over how they are used. Those who own companies, capital or other important assets can influence investment, production and economic development.

Ownership can be spread among many people or concentrated in a small number of actors. How ownership is distributed therefore also affects where economic power lies.

Ownership is therefore not just an economic question. It is also about influence and responsibility for how resources are used. Who should own what everyone depends on is covered on the page [Ownership and Basic Security](<../Ownership and Basic Security/Ownership and Basic Security.md>).

## How does economic power become concentrated?

Through ownership, capital or control over important resources gathering with a limited number of actors. Those who control large economic resources have greater opportunity to influence investments, companies and other decisions.

Power can also be reinforced by economic resources giving access to networks, knowledge and positions that in turn make it easier to influence events.

Competition is usually described as the opposite of concentrated power. It pushes down prices and drives new solutions, and that is a strong argument for the growth advocates. But competition also has winners, and the winners can become so large that they buy up or knock out their competitors. The biggest winners today are multinational groups with subsidiaries in many countries, which makes them hard for any single country both to tax and to regulate. In 2022, around 35 per cent of multinational companies' foreign profits were shifted to tax havens, according to the EU Tax Observatory. That is equivalent to almost ten per cent of the world's corporate tax revenue. In 2021, countries agreed on a global minimum tax of 15 per cent, but in January 2026 they agreed to exempt American groups from important parts of it.

Concentration does not have to mean that anyone formally decides over the whole economy. It can be enough that some actors have much greater opportunity to influence events than others.

## Who actually decides over the economy?

No single actor. The economy is shaped through many decisions and relationships between households, businesses, banks, the public sector and other organisations.

At the same time, the actors have different amounts of power. Ownership, capital, political mandates, knowledge and control over important resources give different opportunities to have influence.

The economy is therefore shaped both by millions of everyday decisions and by decisions from actors with great economic or political influence.

## Who benefits when the economy grows?

Not everyone equally. Economic growth can increase income and create opportunities for investment and development, but the gains are not automatically shared equally.

Who gets a share of the increased resources is influenced by wages, ownership, taxes, investment and how the economy's institutions are designed, among other things. Growth can therefore create more resources while differences between people persist or increase.

Distribution also affects nature. In 2019, the richest tenth of the world's population accounted for about half of all emissions from consumption, and the richest one per cent for 16 per cent. The question of growth therefore cannot be separated from the question of how what is created is distributed, and who causes the pressure.

## How does economic power affect democracy?

Large economic resources can give greater opportunities to influence how society develops, for example investment, opinion-forming and other parts of public life.

At the same time, democracy rests on the principle that people should have an equal opportunity to influence shared decisions. If economic resources become very unevenly distributed, a tension therefore arises between economic power and democratic power.

A sustainable society needs to be able to handle that tension through clear rules, openness, accountability and opportunities for democratic influence.

## So what is the answer, really?

Economic power largely follows access to resources, ownership and capital. The distribution of economic resources is therefore also a distribution of opportunities and influence.

That does not mean all economic differences are a problem. The central question is whether the differences become so large that people's room for action and their opportunities to influence society differ sharply.

A sustainable economic system therefore needs to do more than create resources. It also needs to handle how resources, power and responsibility are distributed, so that the economy can work together with the democratic and social systems.

**So the question is not only how much the economy creates, but who gets to influence what is created and how the resources are used.**
