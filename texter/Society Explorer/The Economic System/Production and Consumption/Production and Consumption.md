## What is the economy actually supposed to produce?

What people and society need, but in practice the economy produces what is in demand and profitable. That covers everything from food, housing and energy to transport, healthcare, education and all kinds of services.

What is produced is not decided by any single actor. Businesses, households and the public sector make many different decisions that together shape production. Demand, prices, rules, access to resources and political priorities all influence the direction.

Production is therefore not just a technical question of what we are able to make. It is also a question of what society chooses to use its resources for.

## How is economic value created?

By using resources, work, knowledge and capital to create goods or services that someone values and is willing to pay for.

But economic value is not the same as the value something has for people or nature. An activity can bring in large revenues while causing damage that nobody pays for, such as emissions, noise or lost nature. Such costs are called **externalities**, because they fall outside the deal between buyer and seller.

So the price does not always tell the whole story of what an activity means for society.

## What determines what is produced and consumed?

Above all, demand and prices. When people buy something, revenue is created that affects what businesses have reason to produce. At the same time, businesses respond to costs, competition, rules and the opportunity to make investments.

Public services are partly steered in other ways, through political decisions and shared funding.

Production and consumption are thus shaped by an interplay between markets, people, businesses, the public sector and political rules. What is in demand and profitable has a big impact, but it is not always what society needs most.

## Why do we consume so much?

Because consumption is driven both by needs and by what we want, and because today's economy is built for it to increase. Consumption is influenced by income, prices, availability, advertising, norms, technology and our own priorities.

When people buy goods and services, businesses get revenue, which contributes to production, income and employment. That is why there is a strong link between economic activity and increased consumption.

At the same time, consumption requires energy, materials and natural resources. The world's material use has more than tripled since 1970, and in rich countries each person uses on average six times as many resources as in poor countries, according to the UN Environment Programme (UNEP). That creates a tension when the development of the economy needs to fit within planetary boundaries.

## How is what is produced distributed?

Through several different mechanisms. Wages distribute income to those who work. Companies' profits go to owners and can be used for investment or other purposes. Taxes and public spending distribute resources in other ways.

Distribution is therefore affected both by the market and by political decisions and institutions.

How resources and income are distributed affects people's economic security and room for action. It also affects what opportunities people have to take part in society and to shape their lives.

## What happens when the economy's use of resources exceeds what nature can sustain?

Then the systems the economy itself depends on are weakened. Energy, materials, water and working ecosystems are preconditions for all economic activity.

If resource use leads to nature's capacity to recover being exceeded, consequences arise that eventually also affect the economy and people's living conditions. Today seven of the nine planetary boundaries have been crossed, and all seven are moving in the wrong direction.

So the problem is not that we use natural resources. The problem arises when use and pressure become greater than the ecological systems can bear in the long term.

## Does everything we buy have to be sustainable?

No. It is the total that counts, not each purchase. It works like a household budget: nobody examines every purchase, but the whole has to add up. A sustainable society sets limits for the whole, and within those limits people and businesses choose for themselves.

The limits already exist, in three forms. The first is **caps** on how much may be emitted or taken out, such as fishing quotas and the EU Emissions Trading System. The cap in emissions trading is lowered step by step, and emissions from the activities covered are now about half of what they were in 2005. The second is prices that include externalities, such as the Swedish carbon tax since 1991. The third is rules for what cannot be priced. Through the Montreal Protocol, almost 99 per cent of the banned ozone-depleting substances have been phased out, and the ozone layer over Antarctica is expected to be restored around 2066.

Doesn't that amount to a planned economy? No. No authority decides what each person should buy. Caps and prices are the market economy's own tools, and many economists favour them precisely because they let the market find the cheapest solutions. In 2019, over 3,500 American economists, among them 28 recipients of the Nobel memorial prize in economics, signed a statement calling for a rising carbon tax. On this point the critics of detailed regulation are right: prices carry more knowledge than any plan can gather.

The tools still have limits. Caps work best for things that can be measured, such as carbon dioxide and fish. The loss of biodiversity is harder to cap, and emissions in other countries from what we import are not counted. The EU has therefore begun to charge for the emissions in imported steel, cement and fertiliser, among other things, from 2026. So the question is not whether limits can be set, but how far they reach and how quickly they can be tightened.

## So what is the answer, really?

The economy does not only produce what we need, but also what is in demand and profitable to produce. That is an important part of how today's economic system works.

The challenge for a sustainable society is therefore not to stop producing or consuming, but to be able to meet people's needs without at the same time crossing planetary boundaries.

That does not require every product to be vetted or anyone to decide what people may want. It requires the whole to fit within the boundaries: caps where the pressure can be measured, prices that include the damage and rules for what cannot be priced. Within those limits, the market can do what it is good at.

**So the question is not whether each purchase is sustainable, but whether the total fits within planetary boundaries.**
