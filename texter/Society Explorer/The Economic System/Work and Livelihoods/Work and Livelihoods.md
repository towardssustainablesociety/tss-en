## Why do people need to work to make a living?

Because today's society is organised so that most people get their livelihood through wages. With our wages we pay for food, housing, energy, transport and other needs.

Work is therefore not just about carrying out a task. It is also part of the economic system that links people's work with production, income, consumption and tax revenue.

But it is not work itself that is necessary for human survival. What needs to exist is food, housing, care, energy, knowledge and other things people need. The question is therefore how society organises the work of creating these things, and how people get a share of the result.

## Why do we have to create jobs?

Because people need an income, and in today's system income comes mainly from work.

At the same time, society needs work to produce goods and services and to keep organisations running. Work therefore has a double function: it is both a way for people to earn an income and part of the production that keeps the economy going.

No company, however, hires people in order to create jobs. Labour is bought when it pays. Since work is taxed considerably more heavily than machines, it is often more profitable to automate or to move production to a country with lower wages.

But that does not mean every conceivable job has to be created. The interesting question is rather which work is actually needed and how society wants to share it out.

## How much do we have to work?

There is no natural answer. How much we work depends on how society organises production, livelihoods, income and welfare.

**Productivity**, meaning how much is produced per hour worked, has risen sharply. In 1930 the economist John Maynard Keynes believed that a hundred years later the standard of living would be four to eight times higher, and that a working week of fifteen hours might then be enough. The first part has broadly come true in the rich countries. The second has not, because higher productivity has mainly been used for more production and consumption rather than more free time.

How working time is distributed is therefore not just a question of individual work ethic. It is also a question of how the economic system is organised and which goals society prioritises.

## Why is work so central to our livelihood?

Because work both provides income and is the way much of society's production and services come about.

The problem arises when the link between work and livelihood becomes so strong that people who cannot work, or are not allowed to, find it hard to make ends meet. Then the labour market becomes decisive for people's security and room for action as well.

That makes work more than an economic question. It affects people's opportunities to shape their lives and take part in society.

## Why is unemployment a problem for society?

Because it hits both the individual and society. For someone who wants to work but cannot find a job, unemployment can mean lost income, less economic security and less room for action.

For society, unemployment means that labour and skills go unused. Production is lower and tax revenue falls, while the need for financial support may rise.

But a society also needs to be able to handle situations where less work is actually needed. If technology makes production more efficient, it is not obvious that the answer is always to create new jobs just to keep the same amount of employment.

## Why can't we share the work that needs doing?

We can, at least partly. If more people share the work, each person's working time can be reduced. Iceland tested shorter working hours without a cut in pay for around 2,500 employees between 2015 and 2019. Productivity and service stayed the same or improved at most workplaces, and today a large majority of Icelandic workers have shorter hours or the right to shorten them.

But it is not as simple as dividing the number of working hours equally. Different jobs require different knowledge, training and responsibility, and some work has to be done at set times.

The question still shows that the division of work is not a given. How much people work and how work is shared out are influenced by wages, working hours, productivity, taxes, rules and how organisations are run.

## What happens when technology means we need less human work?

It depends on how we choose to use the higher productivity. When technology and automation mean that the same amount of goods and services can be produced with less work, society can get more of what is needed without people having to work as much. That is a great opportunity.

But it also raises the question of how the gains from higher productivity are distributed. If they are used mainly for more production and consumption, the need for work stays high, and the pressure on nature increases. If part of them is used instead for shorter working hours, people can have more time for family, leisure, care, education or other parts of life.

So technology does not decide by itself what happens. It is society's way of organising the economy, work and livelihoods that determines how higher productivity benefits people.

## So what is the answer, really?

We do not need to create jobs just for the sake of work. What we need is a society that can provide for people and meet our needs, while the work that is actually needed gets done.

In today's economic system, work is closely linked both to livelihood and to economic activity. That is why it is hard to change the role of work without also changing other parts of the economy, such as income, social security systems and the view of growth.

**The question is not how many jobs we can create, but how we can organise work and livelihoods so that people can live a good life without an ever-growing need for production.**
