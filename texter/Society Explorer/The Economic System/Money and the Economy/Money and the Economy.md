## Why do we need money?

Because it makes it possible to coordinate a society in which everyone depends on each other. With money we can exchange goods and services without swapping directly with each other. Money also works as a way of measuring economic value and of storing value over time.

In a society where people and organisations depend on each other, money therefore becomes a practical tool. We can sell our labour, buy what we need and use money to make investments.

## Where does money come from?

Most money is created by banks when they lend. When a bank grants a loan, it puts the amount into the borrower's account, and there is then new money in the economy. When the loan is paid back, the money disappears again. In Sweden, notes and coins make up only just over one per cent of all money. The rest is money in bank accounts.

This means that the amount of money in the economy broadly grows when loans grow and shrinks when they are paid off. The central bank does not directly control how much money is created, but influences it through the interest rate. Banks' lending is also limited by laws and regulations.

Once the money exists, it circulates between households, businesses, banks and the public sector. Wages become consumption, companies' revenue becomes wages, purchases and investments, and taxes go to public services and come back into the economy. That money is created through loans also means that much of the monetary system rests on expectations of future income.

## Why is there interest?

Because whoever lends money gives up using it themselves and takes a risk. **Interest** is the price of getting access to money now and paying it back later. For the borrower, interest is a cost. For the lender, it is income.

For businesses, loans make it possible to invest before the revenue comes in, for example in machinery, premises or development. An **investment** means that resources are used today for something that will be useful later. But the loan assumes that future income will be enough to cover interest and repayments.

Interest therefore affects decisions by both people and businesses on whether to borrow, save and invest. Higher interest rates make loans more expensive and can dampen investment and consumption. Lower rates make it more attractive to borrow and invest. That is also why central banks use the interest rate to influence the whole economy.

## How do banks make money?

Mainly on the difference between the interest rate on loans and what it costs the bank to borrow the money itself. In Sweden, that difference, the **gross margin**, was 0.81 percentage points for variable-rate mortgages at the end of 2024, according to the financial supervisory authority Finansinspektionen. On a mortgage of SEK 3 million (about EUR 260,000), that comes to just over SEK 24,000 (about EUR 2,100) a year for the bank, before the bank's own costs. That is not much per loan. The profit lies in the loans being many and large.

Banks also have a special position that other companies lack. The state guarantees deposits, and the largest banks are considered too important to be allowed to fail. That makes it safer to lend money to them, and therefore cheaper for the banks to borrow.

It is not strange that banks make a profit. The profit is needed for them to cope with losses in bad times. The question is rather whether the special position should be free. Since 2022, the largest credit institutions in Sweden have paid a risk tax, on the grounds that large banks can cause society great costs in a crisis. The state-owned bank SBAB and member-owned banks also show that banking can be owned in different ways.

## Where does the new money go?

Either to something new or to something that already exists. When a company borrows to build a factory, or when someone borrows to build a house, something new is created. When someone borrows to buy an existing house or shares, something only changes hands, but the price can rise.

An example: Anna sells her house to Bertil for SEK 5 million (about EUR 440,000). Bertil borrows the whole amount, and the 5 million are new money. Anna pays off her old loan of 2 million, and that money disappears. Anna is left with 3 million that did not exist before. The house is the same as yesterday, but there is more money in circulation and more debt for Bertil.

Rising house prices are not counted as inflation, because inflation measures what the things we consume cost. But the money leaks out into the rest of the economy: the seller consumes, owners borrow more against their higher values, and building picks up. Banks prefer to lend against security in houses and property, because it is safer for them.

Those who get the new money first gain the most. Owners and sellers get the higher prices, while first-time buyers and tenants face them. This is called the **Cantillon effect**, after the 18th-century economist Richard Cantillon. New money can thus move wealth from those who come late to those who were early.

## What happens when loans stop growing?

Then the whole economy can slow down. When many people want to pay off at the same time, money disappears from the economy, demand falls and the prices of houses and shares drop. Then even more people want to pay off.

Sweden has experienced this. When the regulation of bank lending was removed in 1985, loans grew rapidly, and much went to property and shares. In about five years, private sector debt rose from 85 to 135 per cent of GDP. Between 1990 and 1993, GDP fell by 6 per cent and unemployment rose from 3 to 12 per cent. The financial crisis of 2008 followed the same pattern in large parts of the world.

The economist Hyman Minsky described how long good times make lenders and borrowers ever bolder, until the system becomes unstable. What is certain is that a system in which money is created through loans works most smoothly when loans are growing. Whether interest in itself forces constant growth, on the other hand, is disputed among economists, and it is not needed for the argument here.

## Can society steer where money goes?

Yes, and it has been done before. Today the economy has brakes, but no steering wheel. The central bank's interest rate and the state budget affect the whole economy at once. They can brake or accelerate, but not choose what should grow and what should shrink.

Before the deregulation, Sweden steered credit directly. The regulated credit market was mainly there to finance newly built and renovated housing, and banks, insurance companies and the national pension fund had agreements to invest money in housing bonds. That is how a large part of housing construction was financed, including during the so-called Million Programme, when a million new homes were built between 1965 and 1974.

The critics are right that the regulation had drawbacks. Loans moved to an unregulated, so-called grey credit market, and in the end it was unclear how much the regulation actually steered. Bringing back the old regulation is therefore not the solution. But there are tools that influence direction without rationing: capital requirements that make some loans more expensive for banks than others, green bonds that raise money for the transition, and public investment in what society needs.

## So what is the answer, really?

Most money is created when banks lend, and it goes where the loans go. Today a lot goes to existing houses and property, which drives up prices without anything new being created.

There is nothing wrong in itself with banks making money or people borrowing for their homes. The problem is that nobody steers the direction. The central bank and the state can brake and accelerate, but not decide what the new money is used for.

A sustainable society does not need to abolish banks or let the state print money. It needs frameworks that make new money go to a greater extent to what society needs, in the same way as caps and prices can steer production within planetary boundaries.

**So the question is not how we slow down the economy, but how we steer what grows and what shrinks.**
