## How dependent is the economy on nature?

**Completely.** All economic activity needs energy and materials, and people depend on working ecosystems, water, food and other contributions from nature. In 2020 the World Economic Forum, which brings together the world's largest companies, estimated that more than half of the world's GDP, around 44 trillion dollars, is moderately or highly dependent on nature.

So the economy cannot function independently of its ecological surroundings. When the conditions nature provides change, so do the possibilities to produce, consume and provide for people.

The dependence is there even when it does not show in economic transactions. Pollination, clean water and a stable climate have no price, but without them no economy works.

## How dependent is the economy on people and social conditions?

Very. The economy needs people who work, consume, run businesses, get an education, care for others and take part in society. It also depends on things like trust, knowledge, health and working social relationships.

If people lack security or the chance to take part, working life, consumption and economic development are all affected. Much of what keeps the economy going, such as care in the home and voluntary work, does not show in economic statistics either.

The economy is therefore not decoupled from the social system. Economic decisions affect people's lives, while people's living conditions affect how the economy works.

## How does the economy affect democracy?

By giving people and organisations different resources and therefore different opportunities to act. Ownership, income and access to capital affect both people's room for action and the ability of different actors to influence how society develops.

At the same time, democracy needs to set rules for how economic power may be used and how shared economic questions are decided.

The economy and democracy therefore affect each other. Economic structures can both create conditions for the democratic system's room for action and limit it.

## How do changes in one societal system affect the others?

Often more than people think, because society's systems depend on each other. A change in the economy can have consequences for people's living conditions, for democracy's ability to make decisions or for the pressure on nature.

In the same way, changes in ecological, social or democratic conditions can affect the economy. A natural resource that becomes scarce can affect production. Changed social conditions can affect the labour force and demand. Political decisions can change the conditions for the economy.

A problem that looks economic therefore often has causes or consequences in other parts of society.

## What happens when economic goals clash with ecological or social needs?

Then the costs often end up somewhere other than the benefits. A form of production can create jobs and income but at the same time put pressure on nature. An efficiency drive can cut costs but also change people's work and security.

Such situations are not unusual. They show that economic goals do not always coincide with social and ecological goals.

When goals clash, society needs to be able to make the consequences visible, weigh different interests against each other and decide which kind of development it wants to promote.

## So what is the answer, really?

The economy is not a free-standing system. It depends on nature, people, social conditions and democratic institutions, while it affects all these areas in return.

Economic problems therefore cannot always be solved by economic measures alone. If a change in the economy creates problems in nature or in people's living conditions, the solution may need to be found in several societal systems at the same time.

A sustainable economic system therefore needs to be able to work within planetary boundaries, contribute to people's well-being and work together with a democratic society.

**So the economy needs to be understood as part of society, not as a system alongside it.**
