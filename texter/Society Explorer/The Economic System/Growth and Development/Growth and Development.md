## Why does the economy have to grow?

It does not have to by any law of nature. But today's economy is built to grow, and so problems arise when it does not.

**Economic growth** means that the value of what is produced in the economy increases over time. Growth can bring higher incomes and more resources and makes it easier to finance investment and public services. Businesses, households and the public sector have therefore in various ways become dependent on the economy continuing to grow.

The dependence has thus arisen largely from how the economic system is organised, not from what people need to live a good life.

## What is it that actually has to grow?

What grows is **GDP**, gross domestic product, meaning the value of all goods and services produced in a country in a year. GDP does not, however, directly say how people's well-being is developing or how great the pressure on nature becomes.

A car crash, an oil clean-up or a longer car commute increase GDP, because they lead to more economic activity. Care for children and relatives that takes place at home, on the other hand, is not counted at all. An economy can therefore grow while some people's living conditions get worse or nature comes under greater pressure.

This is not a new insight. An international commission led by the Nobel economics laureates Joseph Stiglitz and Amartya Sen concluded in 2009 that GDP measures production, not well-being, and that it needs to be complemented with measures of quality of life and sustainability. Economic growth is therefore not the same thing as everything important in society getting better.

## What happens if the economy stops growing?

In today's system, many parts run into problems at the same time. When businesses expect growing demand, it becomes easier to invest and hire, and when the economy grows, incomes and tax revenues rise. If growth slows, businesses become more cautious, demand can fall and employment is affected.

Since work, incomes, investment and public finances are interlinked, weak development spreads quickly. What has been financed in the expectation of continued expansion becomes harder to sustain.

The problem is therefore not that it is dangerous in itself for the economy to stand still. The problem is that today's system has become dependent on it continuing to grow. The real question then becomes why society needs growth to make security, work and investment function.

## Can society function without economic growth?

Yes, in principle. A society does not need to produce more every year for people to be able to live a good life.

But a society organised around continued economic expansion can run into problems if growth fails to materialise. To function without constant growth, several of the dependencies in today's economy need to change.

They include how work, incomes, investment, enterprise and public finances are organised.

## Can we have a better society without producing and consuming more?

Yes. A better society does not have to mean that the amount of goods and services keeps increasing. If development leads instead to better living conditions, security, health, knowledge and well-functioning public services, well-being can increase even without a corresponding increase in production and consumption.

At the same time, people still need to have their needs met, and society needs to produce much of what people need. The question is therefore what is produced, how resources are used and what the economy actually contributes.

## Can the economy grow without the pressure on the planet increasing?

Yes, to some extent, but nowhere near the pace that is needed. When GDP grows while emissions or resource use fall, it is called **decoupling**. If the pressure only grows more slowly than the economy, the decoupling is relative. If the pressure actually falls, it is absolute. Only absolute decoupling reduces the pressure on the planet.

Research shows that absolute decoupling does occur, but rarely and slowly. A review of over 800 studies, published in 2020, found few examples. A study from 2023 examined the eleven rich countries that have managed to cut their carbon dioxide emissions while their economies grew. At the pace they kept, it would take on average over 220 years to cut emissions by 95 per cent. In the meantime they would emit 27 times more than their fair share of what remains if warming is to stop at 1.5 degrees.

For materials and the other planetary boundaries, things look even worse. The world's annual material use has risen from 30 to 106 billion tonnes since 1970, according to the UN Environment Programme (UNEP), and is expected to increase by a further 60 per cent by 2060. Almost all research on decoupling also concerns carbon dioxide, but the climate is only one of nine planetary boundaries, and seven of them have already been crossed. The loss of biodiversity, the overuse of freshwater and the leakage of nitrogen and phosphorus are driven above all by how much land, material and biomass we use. According to UNEP, the extraction and processing of biomass accounts for around 90 per cent of the loss of biodiversity on land.

So decoupling is not a question of yes or no, but of pace and breadth. Even if emissions are decoupled, that only solves one of seven boundaries that have been crossed. The pressure must fall fast enough within all the boundaries, whether or not the economy is growing at the same time.

## Hasn't Sweden already shown that it can be done?

Partly, and it is the growth advocates' strongest argument. Between 1990 and 2018, Sweden's economy almost doubled while carbon dioxide emissions fell by 27 per cent. The reduction has continued, and in 2025 greenhouse gas emissions within the country were about 34 per cent below the 1990 level. That is due among other things to the carbon tax introduced in 1991, to fossil-free electricity production and to district heating replacing oil boilers. It is a real success.

But the picture changes when you count what we consume. Swedes' consumption gave rise to around 7.6 tonnes of greenhouse gases per person in 2023, and a large part of them are emitted in other countries where our goods are made. In 2021, consumption-based emissions were 88 million tonnes, almost twice the 48 million tonnes emitted within Sweden. Consumption-based emissions per person have also fallen, by just over a third since 2008, and Sweden is one of the eleven countries in the 2023 study. So it is Sweden's pace that turns out to be far from enough.

How quickly the pressure falls is therefore not decided by the economy alone. It is decided by political decisions in the democratic system, by what we see as a good life in the social system and by what nature can bear in the ecological system. Sweden shows that decoupling is possible, but not that it is enough.

## So what is the answer, really?

No, society does not have to grow for people to be able to live a good life. It is above all our current economic system that has become dependent on continued growth.

That makes growth a question about the system. If people's livelihoods, businesses' investment and society's finances depend on constant expansion, it becomes hard to choose a different direction of development. And as long as decoupling is as slow as it is today, every per cent of growth increases the demand on how quickly the pressure must fall.

A sustainable society therefore needs to be able to create security, livelihoods and well-being without depending on production and consumption increasing all the time.

**So the question is not how we can have continued economic growth, but how we can develop the economy so that it contributes to a good life within planetary boundaries.**
