## What does it really mean to own something?

Less than you might think. Owning something is not a single right but several: to use it, to exclude others, to take the returns, to sell it and to use it up. Those rights can be divided between different parties.

Sweden already does this. Someone who owns a forest cannot keep others out, because the right of public access (*allemansrätten*) gives everyone access to nature. Someone who owns a lakeside or seaside plot is usually not allowed to build close to the water, because of shoreline protection. And anyone who wants to dam or divert water needs a permit, even on their own land.

Ownership is therefore not a state of nature. It is rules that society has decided. Through democracy, citizens can change the rules for all ownership without owning everything themselves.

## Who should get the value of land and nature?

It depends on where the value comes from. Returns can come from three sources. They can come from work and good management, as when someone runs a business well. They can come from nature or a location being scarce, like the oil under the seabed or a plot in the middle of a city. That return is called **resource rent**, or economic rent. And they can come from prices rising without the owner having done anything.

The strongest case for the returns going to everyone applies to resource rent. Nobody created the oil or the lake view, and the value of a location often comes from what society has built around it, such as roads, schools and public transport.

Norway is the clearest example. The state's net income from oil is transferred every year to a fund, and the state may only spend the fund's expected return, currently estimated at 3 per cent a year. At the end of 2025 the fund was worth around NOK 21.3 trillion (about EUR 1.8 trillion). Resource rent has become shared savings for present and future generations.

## When should society own, and when should it buy services?

That depends mainly on how easy it is to switch supplier. Competition works well where the buyer can switch when the contract runs out, as with cleaning or office supplies. It works less well where the buyer is tied for many years, for example to a building. Then competition only happens once, at the tender. After that the buyer is locked in and finds it hard to negotiate.

In Sweden, the Nya Karolinska hospital in Solna was built and is run in partnership with private companies, with a contract until 2040. The construction risk was handled, and the hospital was finished on time and at the agreed price. But according to the official government inquiry, the contract handled no risks other than the construction risks, and there is no indication that other solutions were investigated. The risk premium was estimated at SEK 4.7 billion (about EUR 410 million), and for the hospital as a whole the region expects a cost of SEK 57 billion (about EUR 5 billion) up to 2040.

Even the Swedish state usually rents its premises. The police rent their new building in Borlänge from the property company Hemsö on a 15-year lease, which the government approved in 2023. Leases longer than six years require the government's consent, precisely because long leases tie the state. The principle becomes: **competition where switching is easy, ownership where switching is hard.**

## Does the state manage what it owns better?

No, not automatically. The critics are right that public ownership does not guarantee good management. Public organisations can become sluggish, and politicians can use them for short-term purposes.

What matters more is the mandate than who the owner is. Hemsö, which leases out the police building in Borlänge, is mostly owned by the Third Swedish National Pension Fund, and so indirectly by the state. But the pension fund's mandate is to give the highest possible return to pensions. The owner is the state, but the mandate is the market's.

There is also a third way between state and market. The economist Elinor Ostrom received the Nobel memorial prize in economics in 2009 for showing that people can often manage shared resources well themselves, with their own rules. In Sweden it exists in common forests, jointly owned property associations, fisheries management areas and cooperatives.

## How can everyone get what they need?

By protecting basic needs from speculation. Housing, water, energy, healthcare and care are needs that are stable and predictable. Everyone needs them, every year. That distinguishes them from the markets for houses and shares, where prices can rise and fall sharply. Researchers call the part of the economy that carries everyday life **the foundational economy**.

One principle is that basic needs are provided at cost price, while speculation may take place outside the basic needs. Sweden already has such rules. By law, charges for municipal water and sewerage may not be higher than what is needed to cover the necessary costs.

Municipal housing companies show that the line can be moved in both directions. In Sweden they were long run at cost, but since 2011 they must be run on business principles, with a public-benefit purpose. Where the line goes is a political choice, not a law of nature.

## Won't it just lead to housing queues?

Yes, if you only put a ceiling on the price and stop building. It is one of the best-known objections in economics, and it is correct. Keeping rents down without new building gives you queues instead of high prices.

Vienna shows another way. The city owns around 220,000 flats, where about one in four residents lives. Together with around 200,000 subsidised flats, according to the city around 60 per cent of residents live in municipal or subsidised housing. It works because the city has been building for a hundred years and keeps building. But even Vienna is behind on new construction, and new municipal flats are more expensive than old ones.

The lesson is that protecting basic needs only works if society at the same time makes sure there is enough of what is needed.

## Who should pay for basic needs?

We ourselves, just as today. Basic needs can be financed through taxes, through charges at cost and through public borrowing for things that last a long time, such as housing and pipes. Green bonds and other ways of directing credit, covered on the page Money and the Economy, can also contribute.

Some economists argue that the state can create money to finance such things directly. That is disputed, and none of the reasoning here relies on it.

What ultimately sets the limit is not money but real resources: labour, materials, energy and planetary boundaries. A home needs builders and cement, however it is financed.

## So what is the answer, really?

Ownership is rules that society decides, not a state of nature. Citizens do not need to own everything in order to steer, but they need to set the rules for what everyone depends on.

Where it is easy to switch supplier, competition works well. Where it is hard, and where the value comes from nature or from what society has built, there are strong reasons for shared ownership or shared management. And whoever the owner is, the mandate determines what gets done.

Basic security then does not rest on the price of your home going up, but on everyone having access to what they need at a reasonable cost. That corresponds to the doughnut in Doughnut Economics: the inner ring, basic needs, is protected from speculation, while the outer ring sets limits for the rest.

**So the question is not who owns everything, but how what everyone needs is protected from speculation.**
