## What can politics actually influence in the economy?

A lot, but not everything. Politics can influence the economy through laws, taxes, public spending, rules, investment and other **policy instruments**. It also decides the conditions that apply to business, work, trade and finance.

But politics does not operate in a vacuum. The economy consists of many actors who make their own decisions, and a country like Sweden also depends on the rest of the world. Political decisions can change the conditions for the economy, but not steer every economic outcome.

## What can politics not influence?

Politics cannot on its own decide how people, businesses and other actors will act. It can create rules and incentives, but decisions are also made on the basis of prices, knowledge, expectations, needs and other circumstances.

Nor can politics ignore the mutual dependencies of the economy and society. Decisions that work in one area can have consequences in other areas.

The room for political action is therefore both considerable and limited.

## Why can't politicians simply decide how the economy should work?

Because a society consists of many actors with different interests, resources and knowledge. Businesses need to be able to make decisions about production and investment, people need to be able to make their own choices and the public sector needs to handle shared needs.

Politics can set the framework for this, but it cannot replace all the decisions that together shape the economy.

That does not mean the economy stands outside democracy. It means that political steering needs to be about both which rules of the game should apply and how society's shared resources should be used.

## How do taxes, benefits, rules and investment steer the economy?

By changing what pays and which resources are available. Taxes affect which incomes and activities are taxed and how much there is for public services. Benefits and allowances affect people's economic security. Rules set limits for what actors may and must do. Public investment directs resources to things like infrastructure and education.

A clear example is the carbon tax, which Sweden introduced in 1991 as one of the first countries in the world. It made fossil fuels more expensive and therefore less profitable, without politics having to decide exactly what each business and household should do.

Policy instruments thus influence economic decisions through both resources and incentives. What effect they have depends at the same time on how people and organisations respond to them.

## Why is work taxed more heavily than machines and natural resources?

Because the tax system was built to finance welfare through work. In Sweden, nearly 60 per cent of tax revenue today comes from taxes on work, about 30 per cent from consumption and just over 10 per cent from capital.

Profit is taxed the same whether it comes from people or machines. But an employee also brings employer's social security contributions of 31.42 per cent of the wage, plus income tax, while a machine does not, and the investment can be written off. If a company replaces an employee with a machine, or moves the job to a low-wage country, the state therefore loses much more in tax than it gets back in corporate tax. No company hires people in order to create jobs either. Labour is bought when it pays.

The growth advocate's objection is that higher taxes on capital and machines make companies move and weaken competitiveness. That is a real risk for a small, export-dependent country. Other paths are therefore being discussed: taxing what puts pressure on nature, such as energy, materials and emissions, more, and work less. This is called **green tax shifting**. Other proposals are a tax on automated production, a so-called robot tax, or on data and digital services.

The point is that today's tax system makes it expensive to use what society has plenty of, people's work, and cheap to use what the planet is short of, energy and materials.

## Who is responsible for the consequences of the economy?

Many, and often nobody fully. Businesses are responsible for their decisions and their activities. Politicians and public institutions are responsible for decisions and rules within their areas. People have influence through their choices and through taking part in democracy.

But economic consequences often do not follow the same boundaries as responsibility. A decision can benefit one actor while costs or problems arise somewhere else, in another country or for future generations.

That is why responsibility also needs to be about the consequences of decisions and about how different actors work together.

## Why is it so hard to change economic systems?

Because the parts have been built up over a long time and depend on each other. Businesses, work, investment, rules, institutions and people's expectations are interlinked.

When one part changes, other parts are affected. People and organisations may also have an interest in the existing system continuing to work as before.

Aviation fuel is a clear example. Since the Chicago Convention came into force in 1947, fuel for international flights has in practice been tax-free, through a web of agreements between countries. The convention only prohibits tax on the fuel already in the tank when the aircraft lands, and since 2003 EU countries have been allowed to tax fuel for domestic flights. Even so, almost no country has done so, and the EU's proposal for a new Energy Taxation Directive would let commercial aviation avoid tax right up to 2035. So it is not the law that blocks change, but that nobody wants to go first.

Change is therefore not just about making a new decision. It is often about changing several linked conditions at the same time and handling the consequences along the way.

## So what is the answer, really?

Politics can influence the direction and the rules of the economy, but it cannot on its own decide over the whole economy. Economic change therefore requires both political decisions and changes in how other actors act.

That makes a long-term approach and a clear direction important. If politics only reacts to short-term problems, it becomes hard to change systems that depend on each other.

Change also needs to be tested, followed up and adjusted. A sustainable economic system can therefore not be built through a single decision, but through a long-term process in which political priorities, knowledge and experience are allowed to shape development.

**So the task of politics is not to steer every part of the economy, but to create the conditions for an economy that contributes to the society we want to develop.**
