## Who holds power in a democracy?

Formally, the people. In Sweden, the Instrument of Government, the most important of the country's fundamental laws, opens with the words "All public power in Sweden proceeds from the people." The people exercise that power by electing the Riksdag, the Swedish parliament, which makes the laws and appoints the government.

But formal power is only part of the picture. **Power** is the ability to influence what happens, and it is also held by those who own, who know, who can reach people or who have time. A democracy needs to understand both the power written into its constitution and the power that is not.

## Who has power without being elected?

Many people, and most of them have it for good reasons. Public agencies apply the laws and hold a great deal of knowledge. Companies decide on investments and jobs. Trade unions and employers negotiate wages. The media choose what becomes news. Researchers and experts shape our picture of what is possible. Organisations and lobbyists put forward the interests of their members and clients.

There is nothing wrong with that in itself. A society needs knowledge, organisation and scrutiny from many directions. The problem arises when some voices become much stronger than others, or when it is not visible who has influenced a decision.

## How does money influence politics?

Mostly indirectly. In Sweden, the parties are largely funded by public party support, and since 2018 they have had to report their income openly to a government agency, Kammarkollegiet. From 1 July 2027 the rules will be tightened, and anonymous donations and most donations from abroad will be banned.

The larger influence goes through other channels: who can afford to hire analysts, communicators and lobbyists, and who can threaten to move jobs and investments. Sweden has so far had no **lobbying register**. From 1 July 2027, anyone who professionally tries to influence members of the Riksdag and government ministers must register and report which issues they have pursued and whom they have contacted.

How economic power is concentrated and distributed is covered in Power and Distribution in the Economic System.

## What protects us against abuse of power?

Power being divided and scrutinised. In Sweden, the Riksdag makes the laws, the government governs the country, and courts and public agencies are independent when they decide individual cases. The opposition, a free press and the principle of public access to official documents provide scrutiny. No one should be able to gather all power in one place.

The American statesman James Madison wrote in 1788 that if men were angels, no government would be necessary. The point is that the system must work even when the wrong people come to power. Protection should not rest on those in power being decent, but on them not being able to do as they please.

## Why is the separation of powers not enough to steer towards a sustainable society?

Because it is built to stop abuse, not to keep a direction. The separation of powers makes it hard for anyone to do wrong, but it also makes it hard for anyone to take responsibility for the whole. Each actor guards its own area, and whatever falls between the areas becomes nobody's responsibility.

That is part of the explanation for why democracy is good at changing its leaders but lacks a way to set a direction and keep to it. How it could be different is covered in Elections and Parties and The Long Term and the Future.

## Don't we need strong leaders who can make quick decisions?

Sometimes quick decisions are needed, and the objection has a point. When everything has to be negotiated and scrutinised, things move slowly, and some crises do not wait.

But experience shows that power without a counterweight rarely stops where it was meant to. According to the research institute V-Dem, around 72 per cent of the world's population live in autocracies, and in 2024 autocracies outnumbered democracies for the first time in 20 years. Leaders there can act quickly, but nobody can stop them when they are wrong. Democracy's slowness is the price of being able to discover mistakes and correct them.

## So what is the answer, really?

Power in a democracy formally proceeds from the people, but it is exercised by many: the elected, employees, owners, experts and the media. The separation of powers and scrutiny protect against abuse, and Sweden is now tightening the rules on party funding and on lobbying.

But a system built so that nobody can do too much also becomes a system where nobody is responsible for the whole. That is the downside of the balance of power.

**The question is not only how we stop power from doing wrong, but who is responsible for making sure it does right.**
