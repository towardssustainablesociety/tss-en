## Why does democracy need shared knowledge?

Because you cannot disagree in a meaningful way without agreeing on certain facts. If we cannot agree on how much emissions have fallen or how many people lack a home, we cannot discuss what should be done about it either.

Democracy is built on the free formation of opinion. But opinions only become a contribution to building society when they can be tested against reality. An open society needs both the freedom to hold opinions and a shared basis from which to hold them.

## Are all opinions worth the same?

Everyone has the same right to an opinion, but not all claims are equally well founded. Freedom of expression gives everyone the right to say what they believe. It does not mean that everything said is an equally good basis for shared decisions.

The strength of democracy does not lie in all claims weighing the same on questions of fact, but in decisions being made on the best available knowledge, in open and accountable ways. The right to an opinion applies to everyone. The right to have one's claims treated as facts has to be earned.

## How do you tell knowledge from belief and conspiracy theories?

By asking how the claim can be tested. **Established science** is built on observations and experiments that others can repeat and examine. It can be wrong, but it has a way of discovering and correcting its errors.

Religious faith and personal convictions provide meaning and values, but they are not made to be tested against measurements and are therefore not enough on their own as a basis for decisions on energy or climate. Ideologies say how society ought to be, and they need knowledge of how it actually is so as not to lose touch with reality. Conspiracy theories look like explanations but avoid being tested: every piece of counter-evidence becomes proof that the conspiracy is bigger than anyone thought.

## Why do falsehoods spread so easily?

Because they are often more surprising and stir stronger feelings than the truth. A large study of news shared on Twitter between 2006 and 2017 found that false news was 70 per cent more likely to be shared than true news. It was people, not bots, who did the spreading.

The platforms amplify this, because they earn money from attention and show what engages people most. On top of that comes **disinformation**: falsehoods spread deliberately to create confusion or distrust, sometimes by foreign states. Since 2022, Sweden has had a Psychological Defence Agency, whose task is to detect such influence and help counter it.

## What happens to long-term issues in the news feed?

They struggle to be heard. News is about what happened today, and conflicts, scandals and clear-cut disputes get most attention. Slow processes such as the loss of biodiversity, growing debts or an ageing population rarely become news until they become crises.

That is why the debate is dominated by individual statements rather than by choices of direction. Assumptions such as that the economy must always grow, or that more jobs are always a goal, are rarely examined, because they are not news. Independent journalism, public service broadcasting and adult popular education (*folkbildning*) therefore have a special task: to make room for the bigger picture.

## Isn't it dangerous to let experts decide what is true?

Yes, if the experts cannot be scrutinised. The objection is important. Experts have been wrong before, they may have interests of their own and they may be blind to what lies outside their field. A society where one group decides what may be said is not a democracy.

But the alternative to rule by experts is not that all claims weigh the same. It is open science: results and methods are published, others can test them and uncertainties are stated openly. Experts provide the evidence. It is citizens and their elected representatives who weigh the evidence against their values and make the decisions.

## So what is the answer, really?

Democracy needs both the free formation of opinion and a shared basis of knowledge. Without freedom it becomes oppression, and without knowledge it becomes noise.

Public debate today struggles to carry the long-term issues. Falsehoods spread more easily than truths, and the news feed rewards conflict over context. That calls for independent journalism, education and open science, but also for each of us to ask how a claim can be tested.

**The question is not what people have the right to believe, but what society can afford to build its future on.**
