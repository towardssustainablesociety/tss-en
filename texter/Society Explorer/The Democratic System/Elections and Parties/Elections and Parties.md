## What are we actually choosing when we vote?

We choose who will lead the country for the next few years, but rarely where the country is heading. Most people take their vote seriously. In Sweden's parliamentary election in 2022, 84.2 per cent of those entitled to vote did so.

But what we choose between are ready-made packages: a party, a list of promises and an expected government. The promises usually concern single issues such as taxes, healthcare, crime or the price of petrol. What the packages together will do to society in twenty years' time is rarely on the ballot paper.

Compare with a company. The owners appoint a management, but they also decide what the company should achieve and check that it happens. In a democracy, citizens are the owners. We appoint the management, but the direction is rarely part of the order. This is where the central argument of the whole Democratic System begins: democracy is good at changing its leaders, but it lacks a way to set a direction and keep to it.

## Why are there so many ideologies, and what do they guard?

Because society has many parts, and most ideologies grew up to defend one of them. They were largely formed in the 19th and 20th centuries, when different groups needed a language for their interests and values.

Put simply, ideologies can be seen as guardians. Liberal and centre-right ideas guard individual freedom and a working business sector. Social democratic and socialist ideas guard security, equality and what we share. Conservative ideas guard continuity and what has proved to work. Christian democratic ideas guard the family and civil society. Green ideas guard nature and future generations.

Each of them has a point. Freedom, security, continuity, community and nature are all needed in a sustainable society. The problem is that no ideology has the main task of weighing the parts against each other. Instead, the balance becomes the result of negotiations between parties, one budget at a time.

Some political movements are less about one part of society and more about who should count as belonging to the community. That question is dealt with under Rights and Responsibilities.

## Who governs between elections?

The government and parliament. They are scrutinised, but almost only on whether they follow the rules. In Sweden, the Riksdag's Committee on the Constitution examines the government's work. The Parliamentary Ombudsmen check that public agencies follow the law. The Swedish National Audit Office examines how the state's money is used. The principle of public access to official documents and a free press mean that almost anything can be scrutinised by anyone.

That is strong protection, and it should not be taken for granted. But the scrutineers ask first and foremost whether power has been used correctly. None of them is tasked with asking whether society as a whole is moving in the right direction.

The exception is the climate. Since 2017, Sweden has had a **climate policy framework** with a Climate Act. Every year the government must report on emissions in the budget, and every fourth year it must present an action plan for how the target of net zero emissions by 2045 will be met. An independent Climate Policy Council assesses each year whether the policy holds up. It is oversight of direction, but only for one of the nine planetary boundaries.

## Don't we already have a vision for society?

In Sweden, yes, in the constitution, but it has no teeth. The first chapter of the Instrument of Government says that the personal, economic and cultural welfare of the individual shall be fundamental aims of public activity. Public institutions shall in particular secure the right to work, housing and education, and promote social care, social security and favourable conditions for good health. They shall also promote sustainable development leading to a good environment for present and future generations.

Read with Doughnut Economics in mind, the likeness is striking. First a social foundation that nobody should fall below. Then an ecological ceiling that no generation may break through. It is the same picture as the doughnut: a foundation to stand on and a ceiling to stay under.

But the article is a **goal-setting provision**. It says what public institutions should strive for, but it is not intended to be legally binding. No government has to report how its policies relate to it, and nobody follows up whether society is getting closer to the goals. The vision exists, but nobody is responsible for it.

Sweden is one example among many. Several countries have environmental or welfare aims in their constitutions, and all UN member states adopted the Sustainable Development Goals in 2015. So TSS does not need to invent anything new. The vision is called **A Sustainable Society**: a good life for all, within planetary boundaries. It suits everyone, because it says where we are going but not how we get there. TSS proposes that it becomes the shared direction that all parties support. The contest can then be about the way there.

## What can we demand of those who want power?

That each party shows its path towards the shared vision, and what that path does to society as a whole. A vision that everyone can say yes to does not separate the parties, and that is not the intention either. The differences should lie in the paths: what comes first, which tools are chosen and who should bear the costs. That is where political competition belongs.

What can be demanded is a kind of **impact statement**. Each party reports what its proposals are expected to do to all four parts of society: the economy, nature, social conditions and democracy itself. Not just to the part the party prefers to talk about.

There are models. In the Netherlands, the independent planning bureau CPB calculates the effects of party manifestos before elections, for the parties that wish to take part. The analysis shows, among other things, the effects on public finances, employment, purchasing power and poverty, and the environmental planning agency PBL makes its own analysis of the environmental effects. Wales has had a law on future generations since 2015. Public bodies must work towards seven shared well-being goals, and a Future Generations Commissioner follows up how it is going.

With its climate framework, Sweden has already tried the design. The next step is to use it for society as a whole and not just for the climate.

## Isn't it for voters to judge the parties, not a public agency?

Yes, and it should stay that way. The objection is important. If an agency were allowed to decide which party is right, it would be a step towards rule by experts, and experts can also be wrong, have interests and have blind spots. The right to vote for a policy that experts dislike is part of democracy.

But the review proposed here decides nothing. It checks that the parties' statements hold up, rather as an auditor examines a company's accounts without deciding its strategy. It is still the voters who decide, but with better information. Nor does a shared vision tie voters' hands. It says where we are going, not which path is right.

The risks must still be taken seriously. Parties may learn to write glossy statements, and a reviewer may acquire a political bias or more power than was intended. The review therefore needs to be open about its methods, have broad political support and be open to scrutiny itself. Sweden's climate framework shows that it can be done: it was passed by a broad majority in the Riksdag.

## So what is the answer, really?

Democracy is good at changing its leaders, but it lacks a way to set a direction and keep to it. That is not because politicians are worse than other people. It is because of how the system is built. Elections are about leaders and promises, scrutiny is about rules, and each ideology guards its own part of society.

The solution is not a new ideology or a new party. The vision, a sustainable society, already has support, in Sweden even in the constitution. What is missing is for each party to report its path there, and for someone independent to check whether the report holds up. Then an election becomes a choice between paths towards a shared goal, and not just a choice between colours.

**The question is not which colour should govern, but whether every colour can show its path towards the same goal.**
