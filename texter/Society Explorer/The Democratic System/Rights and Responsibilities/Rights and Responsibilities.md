## Can the majority decide anything it likes?

No. In a democracy the majority decides, but within limits. The constitution protects rights such as freedom of expression, freedom of assembly, freedom of religion and protection against discrimination, and they also apply to those who belong to a minority or hold unpopular views.

The limits do not only protect minorities. They protect democracy itself. A majority that is allowed to silence the opposition, control the courts or change the electoral rules to suit itself can make sure it never becomes a minority again.

## What do the fundamental laws protect?

The rules of the game. Sweden has four fundamental laws: the Instrument of Government, the Freedom of the Press Act, the Fundamental Law on Freedom of Expression and the Act of Succession. They determine how power is divided, how elections are held and which freedoms and rights apply. The European Convention on Human Rights also applies as Swedish law.

The basic idea is the **rule of law**: public power is exercised under the law. Public agencies and courts must be objective and impartial, and no minister may decide how an agency is to rule in an individual case. Anyone who feels they have been wrongly treated must be able to have their case heard.

## How hard is it to change the constitution?

In Sweden, harder than an ordinary law, but not especially hard. The Riksdag must make two identical decisions with a general election in between, and the proposal must have been put forward at least nine months before the election. Voters thus get a chance to react.

But a simple majority is enough both times. Swedish protection therefore rests largely on tradition and political culture, and this has led to a debate about whether the protection needs to be strengthened. Many other countries require a qualified majority or a referendum.

## How quickly can a democracy be dismantled?

Faster than people think, and rarely by force. According to the research institute V-Dem, around 72 per cent of the world's population live in autocracies, and in 2024 autocracies outnumbered democracies for the first time in 20 years.

The usual pattern today is not coups. It is elected leaders who step by step take control of courts, media and electoral authorities, often entirely within the law. That is why independent institutions matter so much. They are not obstacles to democracy, but what allows it to continue.

## Do future generations have any rights?

They are beginning to get them, through the courts. Sweden's constitution says that public institutions shall promote sustainable development for present and future generations, but that is an aim and not a right that can be claimed in court.

In other countries, courts have gone further. In 2019 the Supreme Court of the Netherlands ruled that the state had to cut emissions by at least 25 per cent compared with 1990 by the end of 2020. Otherwise the state would be in breach of the right to life and the right to private and family life in the European Convention on Human Rights. In 2024 the European Court of Human Rights found that Switzerland had violated the right to private and family life by not doing enough about climate change. The case was brought by an association of older women.

It is a new way of giving the future a voice: today's rights may include protection against the consequences of today's decisions.

## Won't the courts end up governing?

It is a legitimate concern. Courts are not elected, and political trade-offs belong with those who can be voted out. Critics of the climate rulings argue that they shift power from parliaments to judges.

But in the Dutch case, the court did not decide how emissions should be cut. It left that to the government. The court only required the state to do enough to protect its citizens' rights. That resembles the review described in Elections and Parties: checking that the state does what it has committed to, not which path it chooses. The line is still important to watch.

## So what is the answer, really?

Rights and the rule of law are democracy's safety net. They stop a majority from abolishing democracy, and they mean that whoever loses an election can trust that they will get another chance.

But the protection is fragile if it rests only on tradition, and it does not yet cover those who will live longest with the consequences of today's decisions. That is where courts in other countries have begun to move the line.

**The question is not only which rights we have today, but whether they are enough to protect those who come after us.**
