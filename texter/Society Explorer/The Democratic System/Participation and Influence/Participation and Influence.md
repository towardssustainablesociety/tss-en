## Is voting every four years enough?

No, but the vote is the foundation. It is the only influence that is shared completely equally: one person, one vote. In Sweden's parliamentary election in 2022, 84.2 per cent of those entitled to vote did so.

But a vote every few years says little about what you want on individual issues. Between elections, people have influence through associations, trade unions, parties, petitions, responses to public consultations and contact with their elected representatives. Most of what shapes a society happens between elections.

## Why doesn't everyone vote?

Because participation follows living conditions. In Sweden in 2022, 92.8 per cent of eligible voters who stayed in areas with very good socio-economic conditions voted. In areas with major socio-economic challenges, 63.4 per cent voted, and that is where turnout fell most since 2018.

Those who are short of time, money and contacts also find it harder to have influence in other ways. And those who have never felt that it makes any difference stop trying. Formally everyone has the same rights, but in practice some are heard more than others.

## How can you have influence between elections?

In many ways, but with very different effects. Responding to a local council's public consultation can be meaningful, but it can also be a formality if the decision has in practice already been made. Real influence requires coming in early, while the alternatives are still open, and being able to see how the views were taken into account.

The most proven way is still to organise. Associations, trade unions, study associations and parties give a voice to many people at once and teach people how society works. In Sweden, popular movements helped build modern society, and they are still one of democracy's most important schools.

## Why don't we vote directly on more issues?

We can, but referendums have their limits. In Sweden, national referendums are advisory, and they have dealt with big questions such as nuclear power in 1980, EU membership in 1994 and the euro in 2003.

A referendum splits a question into yes or no, and those who vote do not have to weigh the question against everything else that has to be paid for and prioritised. Referendums therefore work best for clear questions of principle and less well when a lot hangs together.

## What is a citizens' assembly?

A group of ordinary people chosen by lot to study an issue in depth and make proposals. They are selected so that together they resemble the population in age, gender, background and place of residence. They hear from experts and different interests, deliberate over several weekends and then present their proposals.

Ireland has done this several times. A **citizens' assembly** of 99 randomly selected Irish citizens discussed abortion in 2016–2017, an issue politicians had failed to handle for decades. The assembly's proposals led to a referendum in 2018, in which 66.4 per cent voted to change the constitution. In France, 150 citizens were chosen by lot in 2019 for a citizens' convention on climate, which put forward 149 proposals to cut emissions by at least 40 per cent by 2030.

Citizens' assemblies do not replace elected assemblies. But they can help when an issue is too deadlocked or too long-term for ordinary politics.

## Don't ordinary people know too little to decide difficult questions?

It is a common objection, and it has a point: nobody has time to study everything. But citizens' assemblies show that ordinary people who are given time, information and the chance to question experts often arrive at well-considered proposals. The Irish assembly took on one of the country's most charged issues and landed on proposals that a clear majority later accepted.

Knowledge is not held only by experts. Someone who lives near a planned mine, cares for a sick parent or commutes every day knows things that are not in the official reports. There is still an important limit: an assembly that is given no real influence risks reducing trust instead of increasing it.

## So what is the answer, really?

The vote is the foundation, but it is not enough. Influence between elections is unevenly distributed and follows living conditions, and much of what is called dialogue gives little real influence.

There are proven ways to do better: letting people in early, showing how views are taken into account, strengthening associations and letting citizens chosen by lot weigh difficult questions. None of this replaces elections, but it can give democracy what it lacks between them.

**The question is not whether people want to take part, but whether their taking part makes any difference.**
