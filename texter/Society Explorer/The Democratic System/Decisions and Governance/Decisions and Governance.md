## Who carries out the decisions that are made?

Almost never those who made them. In Sweden, the Riksdag makes the laws and decides the budget, and the government governs. But it is public agencies, municipalities and regions that do most of the work. Schools, healthcare, care for older people, housing planning and public transport largely lie with municipalities and regions, which have their own elected councils and their own right to levy taxes.

Swedish state agencies are independent. No minister may decide how an agency is to rule in an individual case. That protects against arbitrary decisions, but it also makes the chain from decision to reality a long one.

## Why don't decisions always turn out as intended?

Because every link in the chain interprets, prioritises and adapts. A law can be clear in parliament and unclear in a municipality that is short of money. One goal can clash with another goal within the same agency. And reality changes while the decisions are being carried out.

That is not strange, and it is not always wrong. Local adaptation is often a strength. But it means that a decision needs to be followed all the way, not just made.

## Why does the whole fall between the cracks?

Because responsibility is divided into sectors. In Sweden, the Transport Administration builds roads, the Environmental Protection Agency protects nature and the municipalities plan housing. Each can do a good job within its own area and still contribute to a result that nobody wanted.

A new road, for example, can be profitable according to the Transport Administration's calculations, increase car traffic and so make the climate target harder to reach, while at the same time steering where the municipality builds. Nobody made a wrong decision, but nobody was responsible for the whole. This is sometimes called **silo governance**: each area is steered from the top down, but nothing steers what happens between them.

## What does the state measure to see whether things are going well?

Mostly the economy. GDP, employment and public finances are closely followed and drive much of the debate. Since 2017, the Swedish government has also reported fifteen new measures of well-being in its Spring Fiscal Policy Bill: five economic, five environmental and five social, such as greenhouse gas emissions, trust and life satisfaction.

It is a step, but the new measures get little space compared with GDP. They are reported, but they rarely steer the budget. What is measured but not used has little effect.

## How do we know whether a decision worked?

Often we don't. In Sweden, the National Audit Office examines the state's activities, and agencies follow up their assignments. But follow-up usually concerns whether the money was used correctly and whether the activities were carried out. More rarely does anyone ask what actually changed, for whom and with what consequences for other parts of society.

A learning system needs to be able to say: this did not work, we are changing course. That is difficult in politics, where an admitted failure easily becomes a weapon for the opponent. That is why follow-up is needed that is independent and measured against the goals, not against how much has been done.

## Won't it become a planned economy if the state is to steer towards a whole?

No, and it is important to say so clearly. The objection is based on real experience. The planned economies of Eastern Europe tried to steer everything from a centre and failed, both economically and environmentally.

Steering towards a whole means something else: having shared goals, measuring whether we are getting closer to them and making sure that different decisions do not work against each other. Sweden's climate framework is an example. Parliament has set the goal, the government reports the path and an independent council reviews it, but it is still companies, municipalities and households that decide how. Steering towards a whole is about direction, not details.

## So what is the answer, really?

A decision in parliament is the beginning, not the end. The chain to reality is long, responsibility is divided and what is measured most is the economy. That is why the whole easily falls between the cracks.

What is needed is not more central control, but shared goals, broader measures and follow-up that shows whether we are moving in the right direction. Then everyone can do their part within their own area and still pull in the same direction.

**The question is not how many decisions are made, but whether together they take us where we want to go.**
