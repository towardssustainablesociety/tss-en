## Why is democracy short-sighted?

Because the reward comes at the next election. A term of office is a few years long – four in Sweden – and those in power are judged on what can be seen before the election. A measure that costs something today but pays off in twenty years is hard to win votes on, especially if the benefit goes to someone else.

It is not that politicians are short-sighted as people. Most of them want to do good in the long run. But the system rewards what is visible now, and those who will bear the consequences furthest ahead have no voice.

## Who speaks for those not yet born?

Formally, nobody. Children and future generations will live longest with the consequences of today's decisions on climate, nature, debt and infrastructure, but they cannot vote. Sweden's constitution says that public institutions shall promote sustainable development for present and future generations, but nobody is tasked with speaking for them.

In economics, future income and costs are converted into today's value. In politics there is no overall account of what we leave behind, with the exception of the climate.

## Has Sweden made long-term decisions before?

Yes, several times. The pension system from the 1990s was developed by five parties across the political divide, with the aim of keeping the system stable whoever is in power. Since then the parties have managed it together in a cross-party pensions group.

The climate policy framework from 2017 is another example. It was passed by a broad majority in the Riksdag, sets a target for 2045 and requires every government to report how its policies lead there. What both have in common is that the parties agreed on the framework but went on competing over the content.

## What tools are there for giving the future a voice?

Several have been tried. In Finland, the government has presented a Report on the Future to parliament every electoral term since 1993, and parliament has its own Committee for the Future. Wales has had a law since 2015 that requires public bodies to work for the long term towards seven well-being goals, and a Future Generations Commissioner who follows up how it is going.

Other ideas are that an independent body reviews how new laws affect future generations, or that the future gets its own representative in parliament. Courts have also begun to play a role, which is covered in Rights and Responsibilities.

## Doesn't binding policy for the long term tie the hands of future voters?

That is the most important objection, and it must be taken seriously. Today's voters have no right to decide for tomorrow's. If a goal is written in so that it cannot be changed, part of democracy is taken away.

But the tools that work do not lock anything in. The Riksdag can change the climate framework, and the parties can leave the pensions agreement. What the tools do is make sure that changes of course cannot happen quietly. A government that wants to take a different path may do so, but it must say so openly and report the consequences. It is the same difference as between a shared vision and a locked policy: the vision says where we are going, not which path is right.

## So what is the answer, really?

Democracy is short-sighted by design, not by ill will. Elections come every few years, and those who will bear the consequences furthest ahead have no voice.

But Sweden has shown that it is possible to make decisions that hold over several terms of office, when the parties agree on the framework and compete over the content. The pension system and the climate framework are examples. What is missing is the same arrangement for society as a whole, not just for individual issues.

**The question is not whether democracy can think long-term, but whether we build that into the system instead of hoping for it.**
