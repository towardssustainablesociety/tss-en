## Are nature's resources running out?

Some are, but so far that is rarely the biggest problem. Oil, metals and minerals exist in limited quantities, but often in larger quantities than people thought a few decades ago. New deposits and new methods keep being found.

What is running out instead are the renewable resources, when they are used faster than they can regenerate. According to the UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), almost 38 per cent of the world's assessed fish stocks were overfished in 2021. Groundwater is pumped up faster than it is replenished, and fertile soil erodes away. What ought to last for ever becomes finite when we eat into the capital itself.

Some resources run out in a different way. The atmosphere does not run out, but its capacity to absorb carbon dioxide without the climate changing does.

The picture also changes when you look ahead. The rich countries have built their cities, roads and machines with large amounts of metal. If the rest of the world is to reach the same material standard while the energy system is transformed, demand will rise sharply. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), demand for lithium could increase eightfold and for graphite fourfold by 2040 if the world is to reach net zero emissions. The question then is not only what is in the bedrock, but how quickly new mines can be opened and what they do to nature and people where they are located.

## Doesn't the market solve scarcity with higher prices?

Often, yes. It is one of the growth advocates' strongest arguments. When a resource becomes scarce, its price rises. Then it becomes profitable to look for new deposits, use the resource more efficiently or replace it with something else. A well-known example is the 1980 wager between the economist Julian Simon and the biologist Paul Ehrlich on the price of five metals. Ehrlich believed prices would rise as the metals became scarcer. They fell, and Simon won.

But the mechanism only works for things that have a price. Fish in the sea, groundwater, pollinators and the atmosphere's capacity to absorb carbon dioxide usually have no price, or a price that does not reflect the damage. There the market says nothing until it is too late, as when the cod disappeared off Newfoundland. Who owns nature, and who should receive its value, is discussed on the page [Ownership and Basic Security – The Economic System](<../../The Economic System/Ownership and Basic Security/Ownership and Basic Security.md>).

The market is therefore good at handling scarcity of what is sold, but poor at protecting what is not.

## How much do we use?

More than ever, and the amount is growing. According to the UN's International Resource Panel, global extraction of materials has roughly tripled in fifty years, from around 30 billion tonnes in 1970 to 106 billion tonnes today. That is on average around 35 kilos per person per day. Without a change in policy, extraction is expected to increase by a further 60 per cent by 2060.

According to the same report, the extraction and processing of materials account for over 60 per cent of the world's greenhouse gas emissions. Agriculture and forestry account for around 90 per cent of the loss of biodiversity and the water stress caused by land use.

## Why does resource use keep increasing?

Because it follows the economy. More production and consumption require more materials and energy, even when each product becomes more efficient. Efficiency makes things cheaper, and then we often buy and use more. This is called the **rebound effect**: part of the gain is eaten up by increased use.

Cars are an example. Engines have become much more efficient, but cars have at the same time become bigger and heavier. Another example is cities. Housing and infrastructure for a growing urban population require enormous amounts of stone, sand, cement and steel.

That is why the question of resources cannot be separated from the question of growth.

## Who uses the resources?

They are very unevenly distributed. According to the UN Resource Panel, high-income countries use six times as much material per person as low-income countries, and cause ten times as much climate impact per person.

This means the world has two different resource problems. In poor countries, many people need to use more resources to get housing, electricity, clean water and enough food. In rich countries the challenge is the reverse: to get a good life out of less. And the two problems are connected. If everyone used as much material as in high-income countries, extraction would be considerably larger than today, and today's extraction is already too large.

## Is it possible to live well with less?

Yes, but it requires changing how we do things. A study in Nature Sustainability in 2018 compared a large number of countries and found that no country today meets its people's basic needs with a level of resource use that would be sustainable if everyone lived that way. Even so, the researchers judge that basic needs such as food, sanitation and an end to extreme poverty can probably be met for everyone within planetary boundaries.

Much of the resource use in rich countries goes to things that are used little: cars that stand still most of the day, clothes worn a few times and living space that stands empty. There are great opportunities there to get the same benefit from less material.

So it is not about living worse, but about getting more quality of life out of every tonne of material.

## So what is the answer, really?

Resources rarely run out in the way people imagine, with an empty mine or a dried-up oil well. But if everyone is to live as people do in rich countries, even metals and minerals become a bottleneck. What is already running out today is nature's capacity to renew itself and to absorb what we leave behind. Fish, forests, soil, water and atmosphere are the most threatened resources, and they are exactly the ones the market finds hardest to protect.

At the same time, resource use keeps growing in step with the economy, and it is very unevenly distributed.

**So the question is not whether resources will last, but whether nature can bear what we take out and release.**
