## Isn't population growth the big problem?

Both the number of people and how much each person uses matter. The pressure on nature is basically the two factors multiplied together. More people mean more pressure, and more people living as people do in rich countries mean much more.

Today use is very unevenly distributed. According to Oxfam, the richest one per cent of the world's population, around 77 million people, account for roughly as large a share of consumption-related emissions as the poorest two thirds, over five billion people. The total pressure is not even greater simply because most people still use very little.

That is no comfort. Billions of people have a right to a better life and are striving for it, and the model is often the lifestyle of the rich world. If everyone lived like the Swedes, we would need about four planets according to the Global Footprint Network. With more people and the same model, the problem grows quickly.

So the question is not population or consumption. It is the combination that counts.

## How big is the footprint of a country like Sweden?

Big, even though Sweden is often described as a pioneer. According to the Swedish Environmental Protection Agency, Swedes' consumption causes emissions of around 7.6 tonnes of greenhouse gases per person per year. To meet the climate targets, the global average needs to come down to no more than about one tonne per person by 2050.

Another measure is the **ecological footprint**, which compares how much nature we use with how much has time to regenerate. According to the Global Footprint Network, Sweden had used up its share of the year's resources as early as 10 April in 2025. For the world as a whole, that day fell on 24 July.

## Why is the footprint so big when Sweden has such clean electricity?

Because most of Swedes' emissions happen in other countries. According to the Swedish Environmental Protection Agency, 64 per cent of consumption-based emissions in 2023 arose abroad, when clothes, electronics, food and other goods were made and shipped to Sweden.

The usual climate statistics only count emissions within a country's borders. There Sweden looks good, because it has a lot of hydropower, nuclear power and wind power. **Consumption-based emissions** instead count the emissions against the buyer of the goods, wherever they were made.

Things are still moving in the right direction. Consumption-based emissions per person in Sweden have fallen by around 36 per cent since 2008. But the pace needs to increase sharply to get down to one tonne.

## Do my own choices make any difference?

Yes, but only up to a point. A few choices account for a large part of a Swede's climate impact: flights, driving, meat and dairy products, the size of one's home and how much new stuff we buy. Those who change them can reduce their footprint considerably.

At the same time, a large part of the footprint lies outside the individual's control. How electricity is produced, how public transport works, what is in the shops and how homes are built are decided by companies and political decisions. Nobody can on their own opt out of the emissions that are built into society.

Personal choices also matter in another way: they influence norms. When many people change their behaviour, it becomes easier for others to follow, and for politicians to make decisions.

## Why is it so hard to live sustainably?

Because society is built for something else. The sustainable choice is often more expensive, more complicated or less socially accepted. It can be cheaper to fly than to take the train and easier to buy new than to repair, and in the countryside the car can be the only reasonable way to get to work.

Prices also rarely reflect the damage that production causes. Those who choose the environmentally friendly option often pay more, while those who choose what does harm avoid paying for their impact. That is not primarily a failing in people, but in the rules of the game.

## What happens when the gap becomes too large?

Then tensions grow. When a few use a large share of the Earth's resources while others lack the bare necessities, a conflict arises that is not only about the environment but about justice. It is reinforced by the fact that those who have contributed least to the problems are often the first to be hit by drought, floods and rising food prices.

You can compare it with a thunderstorm. The tension between cloud and ground builds up quietly, for a long time without anything being noticed. But when it becomes large enough, there is a discharge. In the same way, a growing gap in access to the Earth's resources can build up until it breaks out in conflict, displacement or political instability.

The difference from a thunderstorm is that people can see the tension coming and do something about it before it strikes. That requires the gap to become visible and for there to be ways of dealing with it, both within countries and between them.

## What size of footprint is reasonable?

One that would work if everyone had it. That is the idea behind both the planetary boundaries and Doughnut Economics. Everyone should be able to have their basic needs met without the total pressure becoming greater than the planet can bear.

Today we are far from that. According to the Global Footprint Network, humanity as a whole uses natural resources equivalent to about 1.8 planets. Rich countries are far above their share, while many poor countries are below it but lack things people need.

## So what is the answer, really?

The pressure on nature depends both on how many of us there are and on how much each of us uses. Today the total pressure is kept down by most people using little. That only works as long as they stay poor, and that is neither reasonable nor likely. Sweden is one of the countries that use the most, even if it does not show in the usual statistics.

Personal choices matter, but they are not enough. The sustainable needs to become the easy and obvious choice. And the gap between those who use the most and those who have the least needs to narrow, or tensions will grow. That requires changes in economics and politics.

**So the question is not who should be blamed, but how we make it possible for everyone to live well with a footprint the planet can bear.**
