## How is the planet doing, really?

Worse than just a few decades ago, and in several areas at once. The scientists behind the planetary boundaries have identified nine processes that together keep the Earth stable. According to their latest assessment, the Planetary Health Check 2025, seven of the nine are outside the safe zone: climate change, biodiversity loss, land-system change, freshwater, nitrogen and phosphorus flows, novel entities (new chemical substances) and now also ocean acidification.

2024 was the warmest year on record, around 1.55 degrees warmer than before industrialisation according to the World Meteorological Organization (WMO). The wildlife populations that scientists monitor have declined by an average of 73 per cent since 1970, according to WWF's Living Planet Report.

Only two boundaries have not been crossed: the ozone layer and atmospheric aerosols. They show something important, namely that it is possible to turn things around.

## What does it mean that a boundary has been crossed?

That we have left the zone where scientists judge that the Earth system will very probably stay stable. It does not mean that something collapses the next day. A planetary boundary is more like a speed limit than a cliff edge. The risk increases the further beyond the limit you go, even if it is not possible to say exactly when the accident will happen.

The boundaries are set with a safety margin. Beyond them lies a zone of increasing risk and further out a zone of high risk. According to the Planetary Health Check 2025, the situation is getting worse for all seven boundaries that have been crossed.

## Isn't this exaggerated? Hasn't the environment improved?

In many places it has, and that should not be played down. In Sweden, the air in cities is cleaner than fifty years ago, the acidification of lakes has decreased and the ozone layer is healing. Those are real successes.

But they are often about local problems with clear sources, such as a factory chimney or an outflow pipe. The global pressures, such as greenhouse gas emissions, the loss of species and the extraction of materials, are instead continuing to grow. Part of the improvement in rich countries is also because dirty production has moved to other countries, from which we then buy the goods.

So both things are true. The environment has improved where we have tackled the problems, and got worse where we have not.

## Why do people talk about both climate and biodiversity?

Because they are the two processes that most govern the state of the Earth, and because they are connected. The climate determines the living conditions of species. At the same time, living nature, such as forests, peatlands and oceans, is crucial for the climate because it stores carbon.

When biodiversity declines, nature finds it harder to adapt to a changing climate. When the climate changes quickly, species find it harder to survive. The problems therefore reinforce each other. That is why it rarely works to solve one at the expense of the other, for example by replacing species-rich forest with fast-growing plantations to store carbon.

## Why isn't it noticed more in everyday life?

Because the change is slow measured in a human lifetime but fast measured in the Earth's history. Each generation tends to see the nature it grew up with as normal. This is called **shifting baselines**. Someone who has never seen a Baltic Sea full of cod does not miss it.

Much of the change also happens far away, in the depths of the oceans, in the tropics or in the atmosphere. And the rich world has the resources to soften the consequences, for example by importing food when the harvest fails. Those who are hit first and hardest are often those who have contributed least.

## How fast is the change happening?

Faster than almost anything we know from the Earth's history. The concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere has risen from around 280 parts per million before industrialisation to just over 425 today. According to the UN's climate panel, the IPCC, the concentration is higher than at any time in at least two million years.

Species are also disappearing at an unprecedented rate. According to IPBES, the UN's science panel on biodiversity, the rate of extinction is tens to hundreds of times higher than the average over the past ten million years.

What took nature thousands of years to build up can therefore change in a few decades. It is the speed, more than the change itself, that makes the situation serious.

## So what is the answer, really?

The planet is not doing well. Seven of the nine planetary boundaries have been crossed, and for all seven the trend is going the wrong way. This is not the alarm of a few individual scientists, but an overall picture from thousands of measurements and studies.

But the picture is not hopeless. The ozone layer shows that even global problems can be turned around when the world makes up its mind. The difficulty is that today's major pressures are built into how we produce food, energy and goods.

**So the question is not whether the planet is out of balance, but whether we can change course in time, before the imbalance becomes hard to reverse.**
