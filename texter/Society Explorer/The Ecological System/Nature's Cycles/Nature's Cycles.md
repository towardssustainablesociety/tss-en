## What is meant by nature's cycles?

That nature does not waste. What one part of nature leaves behind becomes a resource for another. Plants take up carbon dioxide and nutrients, animals eat the plants, and when plants and animals die they are broken down by fungi and bacteria into nutrients that new plants can take up.

The same goes for water, carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus. They move constantly between air, soil, water and living organisms. The only thing added from outside is energy from the sun. Everything else is used again and again.

## Why do the cycles matter for society?

Because almost everything we depend on is part of them. Fertile soil exists because nutrients are returned to the land. Clean water exists because water is cleaned as it runs through soil and wetlands. A stable climate requires the carbon cycle to be reasonably in balance.

The cycles are also nature's way of dealing with waste. What is broken down becomes new nutrients. As long as the load is moderate, nature does that work for free, and without us noticing.

## How have humans affected the cycles?

Heavily, above all by moving substances faster than nature can handle them. The clearest example is carbon. When we burn coal, oil and gas, we move carbon that has been stored in the bedrock for millions of years out into the atmosphere. Emissions from fossil fuels were around 38 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide in 2025, a new record according to the Global Carbon Project.

Nitrogen is another example. Synthetic fertiliser makes it possible to feed about half of the world's population. But much of the nitrogen and phosphorus does not end up in the crops. It runs off into lakes and seas. Both the nitrogen and the phosphorus cycles are now outside the planetary boundaries.

## Can't nature take care of it on its own?

Partly. Oceans, forests and soil currently take up about half of the carbon dioxide that humans emit. Without that help, the climate would have changed considerably faster. But the rest stays in the atmosphere, where the concentration is now just over 425 parts per million (ppm), compared with around 280 before industrialisation.

The Baltic Sea shows what happens when there are too many nutrients. Eutrophication causes algal blooms, and when the algae decompose, the oxygen is used up. According to the Swedish Meteorological and Hydrological Institute (SMHI), around 18 per cent of the seabed in the Baltic Proper was completely without oxygen in 2024, and around a third suffered from oxygen deficiency. There is almost no life left on the bottom there.

Nature's capacity to deal with what we release is therefore large, but it is not unlimited.

## Isn't recycling the solution?

Recycling helps, but it does not go very far today. According to the Circularity Gap Report, only 6.9 per cent of the materials entering the world economy in 2021 were recycled. The share has also fallen, because total material use is growing faster than recycling.

Much of the material cannot be recycled. Fossil fuels are burned, food is eaten and building materials stay in houses and roads for decades. Recycling also requires energy, and materials often lose quality with each round.

A circular society is therefore less about recycling more and more about using less, using things for longer and designing products so that they can be repaired, rebuilt and taken apart.

## What could a circular society look like?

Basically like nature itself: powered by renewable energy, with materials that go round instead of becoming waste. The nutrients in food are returned to the fields, for example by recovering phosphorus from sewage sludge. Products are shared, rented, repaired and upgraded instead of thrown away. Buildings are used for a long time and built so that their materials can be used again.

Much of this already exists, but on a small scale. What holds it back is usually not technology. It is that it is still cheaper to take new raw materials and to pollute than to take care of what we already have. That is a question of the rules of the game, and therefore of economics and politics.

## So what is the answer, really?

Nature has managed for billions of years by using everything again. Society has been built on the opposite principle: take, use and throw away. As long as we were few and used little, it was hardly noticed. Today we move such large amounts of carbon, nitrogen and materials that nature's cycles cannot keep up.

That does not mean we have to stop using nature. It means that society's flows need to be connected with nature's, so that what we leave behind becomes a resource again instead of a burden.

**So the question is not how much we can recycle, but how we build a society that works as a cycle.**
