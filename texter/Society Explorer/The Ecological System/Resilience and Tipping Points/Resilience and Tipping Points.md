## Can't nature always recover?

Often, but not always. Nature has a great capacity to recover after disturbances. A forest grows back after a fire, and a lake can become clear again when the pollution stops. That capacity is called **resilience**: a system's ability to withstand stress, adapt and keep its important functions.

But resilience has limits. If the pressure becomes too great or lasts too long, the system can tip over into a completely different state, which is then stable in its own way. Removing the pressure is then not enough to get back what was there before.

## What is a tipping point?

A tipping point, or threshold, is the point where a small change has large and self-reinforcing consequences. Think of a chair you lean back on. For a long time nothing much happens, but when the centre of gravity passes a certain point the chair falls, and then it cannot be tilted back.

In nature it can be a lake that goes from clear water to murky and dominated by algae, or a rainforest that becomes so dry that it turns into savannah. What makes tipping points dangerous is that the change beyond the threshold drives itself. A drier forest burns more easily, and a forest that burns becomes drier.

## Has it already happened?

Yes. One of the best-known examples is the cod off Newfoundland in Canada. The stock was fished ever harder for several decades. By 1992 the spawning cod had shrunk to around one per cent of its former size, and Canada stopped the fishery. Almost 30,000 people in Newfoundland and Labrador lost their jobs.

When the fishery was stopped, the cod did not come back as many had hoped. It was more than fifteen years before the stock began to grow in earnest, and it is still far from its old levels. In the Baltic Sea, the eastern cod stock has gone a similar way, and fishing for it was stopped in 2019.

Coral reefs are a current example. According to the Global Tipping Points Report 2025, tropical coral reefs are already passing their tipping point at today's warming of around 1.4 degrees.

## How long does the damage last?

Sometimes much longer than whatever caused it. Stopping is then not enough.

The climate change caused by carbon dioxide emissions is largely irreversible for around 1,000 years after the emissions stop. Temperatures do not fall significantly, even though the concentration in the air slowly declines.

PFAS are synthetic substances that have been used in firefighting foam, among other things. They are extremely hard to break down, and some probably never break down in nature. Today they are found more or less everywhere, even in polar bears in the Arctic. In Ronneby in Sweden, the drinking water was contaminated by firefighting foam from an airfield. After people stopped drinking the water, it still took around three to five years for the levels in their blood to halve.

Spent nuclear fuel is a different kind of example, because it is meant to be kept away from nature. In the review of the final repository at Forsmark in Sweden, the risk of spreading was assessed over 100,000 years. Whatever one thinks of nuclear power, it shows how far into the future a decision can reach.

## Why is the damage noticed so late?

Because a system heading for a tipping point often looks stable right up to the end. Resilience works as a buffer that hides the growing stress. The cod was caught in large quantities until it was almost gone, partly because fishing was concentrated in the places where it was still found.

That is why ordinary measurements can deceive us. That something still works says little about how close to the limit it is. Scientists therefore look for early warning signs, for example a system recovering more and more slowly after small disturbances.

## Which tipping points worry scientists most?

Those that affect the whole Earth system. The Global Tipping Points Report 2025 points, among other things, to the polar ice sheets, the Amazon rainforest and the great ocean current in the Atlantic, AMOC, which carries heat to northern Europe. According to the report, AMOC risks collapsing at less than two degrees of warming. That would bring considerably colder winters in north-western Europe.

What makes them especially serious is that they can affect each other. Melting ice can weaken ocean currents, which in turn affect the rains over the rainforest. Scientists speak of a risk of cascades, where one tipping point triggers the next.

## How can we strengthen nature's resilience?

By reducing the pressure and preserving diversity. A system with many species and different habitats has more ways of coping with a disturbance. A mixed forest withstands an insect attack better than a plantation of a single tree species.

It is also about giving nature margins, that is, not using a system right up to the limit of what it can withstand, because we rarely know exactly where the limit lies. The same principle applies to society. A society that depends on a few resources, supply chains or crops is more vulnerable than one that has breadth and reserves. Social cohesion, too, can be worn down and reach a tipping point. That is covered on the page [Tensions and Conflicts](<../../The Social and Cultural System/Tensions and Conflicts/Tensions and Conflicts.md>).

## So what is the answer, really?

Nature is resilient, but not infinitely so. The danger is not that it changes, but that it can change suddenly, and sometimes for good, when a limit is passed. The cod off Newfoundland and the coral reefs show that this is not theory.

Since tipping points are hard to see in advance, it is wise to keep margins. Waiting for clear evidence often means waiting until it is too late.

**So the question is not how much nature can take, but how great a risk we are prepared to take when we do not know where the limit lies.**
