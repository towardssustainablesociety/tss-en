## Is anything moving in the right direction?

Yes, more than you might think. The ozone layer has been healing since the world banned the substances that were destroying it. Almost 99 per cent of them have been phased out. According to the UN's expert panel, the ozone layer is expected to be restored around 2040 over most of the world and around 2066 over Antarctica.

Renewable energy has become cheap. The cost of electricity from large solar parks has fallen by around 90 per cent since 2010, according to the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA). In Sweden, consumption-based emissions per person have fallen by over a third since 2008.

So change is possible. The problem is that it is happening too slowly compared with how fast the pressure is growing.

## Can technology solve the problems?

Partly, and it cannot be done without technology. Renewable energy, electric cars, heat pumps, more efficient industry and new materials are necessary parts of the transition. The growth advocates' hope is that technology will make it possible to keep growing while the pressure falls. This is called **decoupling**.

It has happened in some countries when it comes to carbon dioxide. But a review of over 800 studies, published in 2020, found that examples of real decoupling are few and that the reductions are nowhere near the pace that is needed.

So technology is needed, but so far it has not been enough to reduce the total pressure while the economy is growing at the same time.

## Do we need more energy?

It depends on what the energy is to be used for. Fossil-free energy is needed to replace coal, oil and gas in industry, transport and heating. But how much energy a society needs is not a law of nature. It is the result of how we build cities, produce food, travel and consume.

Becoming fossil-free is not enough to become sustainable either. Fossil fuels mainly drive climate change and ocean acidification. The loss of biodiversity, the overuse of freshwater and the leakage of nitrogen and phosphorus continue even if the factories run on clean electricity. Unsustainable production does not become sustainable by becoming fossil-free, only less harmful to the climate.

Research therefore increasingly points to demand. The UN's climate panel, the IPCC, judges that changes in how we live, eat and travel could cut emissions by 40–70 per cent by 2050. The point is that people need heat, food and transport, not energy as such. A study from 2020 calculated that a decent life for around ten billion people could be achieved with less than 40 per cent of today's global energy use, if consumption changes and the large gaps narrow.

Energy that is expanded without anyone asking what it is for therefore risks keeping the same unsustainable production alive, only with a new source of energy. The question is not only how much energy we can produce, but what we need it for.

## Is the circular economy enough?

No, but it is an important part. The circular economy reduces the need for new raw materials. But as long as total material use keeps growing, recycling can never catch up.

The biggest gain often lies earlier in the chain: not producing what is not needed and using what already exists for longer. Recycling is the last step, not the first.

## Can nature be restored?

Yes, in many cases, if it is given time and space. Wetlands can be recreated, watercourses can get back their natural channels and depleted fish stocks can recover when fishing is limited. In Sweden, lakes that were heavily acidified have come back to life since sulphur emissions fell.

In 2024 the EU adopted a Nature Restoration Law. Under it, restoration measures must cover at least 20 per cent of the EU's land and sea areas by 2030, and all ecosystems in need of restoration by 2050.

But restoration is slow and expensive compared with not destroying in the first place. And what has passed a tipping point does not always come back.

## What should grow and what should shrink?

It may be the most important question in the transition. What needs to shrink is what puts the most pressure on nature: fossil fuels, wasteful production, short-lived products and activities that destroy habitats.

What can grow is what gives quality of life with little pressure: care, education, culture, knowledge, repair, sharing, renewable energy and the restoration of nature. Many of these activities require a lot of work but few materials.

So the transition does not have to mean that everything becomes smaller. It is about changing the composition of the economy, so that what grows is what the planet can bear.

## Why is the transition so slow?

Because today's system is built into everything from infrastructure and ownership to habits and jobs. Power stations, roads, housing estates and factories are built to be used for decades. Companies and countries that profit from the way things are have strong reasons to slow down change.

Moreover, the costs of the transition are visible and come now, while the gains are often spread out and come later. That makes it politically difficult. The pace of the transition is therefore largely decided in the economic and democratic systems, not in the ecological one.

## So what is the answer, really?

There are real successes. The ozone layer is healing, renewable energy has become cheap and nature can be restored. The problem is not that the solutions are missing, but that they are being introduced too slowly while the total pressure keeps growing.

Technology, fossil-free energy and cycles are needed, but they are not enough on their own as long as the economy is built on using more and more. The transition is therefore also about what should grow and what should shrink.

**So the question is not whether the transition is possible, but whether we choose to make it at the pace that is needed.**
