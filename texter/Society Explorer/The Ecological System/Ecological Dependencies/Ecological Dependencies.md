## Is society really dependent on nature?

Yes, fundamentally and completely. Food, water, air, energy and most of the materials we use come from nature or depend on nature's processes working. That is also true of a modern, high-tech economy.

The dependence is easy to see when it comes to raw materials such as timber, fish or ore. It is harder to see when it comes to processes that go on quietly: soil staying fertile, water being cleaned as it runs through soil and wetlands, crops being pollinated and the climate staying reasonably stable.

## Hasn't technology made us less dependent?

Partly, but less than it seems. Technology has made us better at *using* nature. We harvest more per hectare, clean water in treatment plants and move goods all over the world. But technology has not removed the dependence. Often it has only moved it further away from us and made it harder to see.

Pollination is a good example. World food production has increased while many pollinating insects have declined. It can look as if pollinators are not needed. But over the same period, the cultivation of crops that need pollination has increased sharply, by around 300 per cent in fifty years according to IPBES, the UN's science panel on biodiversity. So the dependence has grown, not shrunk.

## How much of the economy depends on nature?

A large share. The World Economic Forum has estimated that more than half of the world's total GDP, around 44 trillion dollars, is moderately or highly dependent on nature. That applies above all to agriculture, food and construction. Animal pollination alone contributes to harvests worth 235–577 billion dollars a year.

The figures are estimates and should be read as orders of magnitude. But they show that nature is not a side issue for the economy. It is part of its foundation.

## Why doesn't the dependence show up in the economy?

Because most of what nature contributes has no price. Nobody sends an invoice for pollination, for a wetland cleaning water or for soil forming new humus. Such contributions are often called **ecosystem services**. They are not included in GDP, and what is not counted is easily forgotten when decisions are made.

This means nature can deteriorate for a long time without it showing in economic statistics. The cost only becomes visible when a function stops working: when harvests fail, when water has to be cleaned artificially or when floods become more common. By then it is often higher than it would have cost to preserve the function.

## What happens when nature deteriorates?

The consequences spread through society. First, activities that depend directly on nature are hit, such as farming, forestry and fishing. Then it is felt in food prices, employment, public finances and people's living conditions.

It is not a distant risk. In 2019, IPBES estimated that around three quarters of the Earth's land surface has been significantly altered by humans and that around one million species are threatened with extinction. The more functions that are weakened at the same time, the harder it becomes for nature, and for society, to recover.

## Why is this not just an environmental issue?

Because the dependence runs through all the societal systems. Economic decisions determine how hard nature is pressed, while the state of nature affects the economy. People's health and living conditions are affected by both. When resources become scarcer or nature changes quickly, interests are set against each other. That tests the democratic system's ability to make shared and long-term decisions.

Ecological problems therefore cannot be solved by environmental policy alone. They need to be dealt with where decisions are actually made: in the economy, in politics and in everyday life.

## So what is the answer, really?

Society is not just dependent on nature. It is built on it. Technology and economic development have changed how we use nature, but they have not made us independent of it. If anything, the dependence has become greater and more invisible.

It is precisely the invisibility that is the problem. As long as nature's contribution does not show in how we measure success, we can wear down the foundation and still believe that things are going well.

**So the question is not whether we can afford to take nature into account, but whether we can afford not to.**
