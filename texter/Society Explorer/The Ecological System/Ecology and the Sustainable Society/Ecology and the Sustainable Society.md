## What does it mean for society to be ecologically sustainable?

That society can go on working without destroying what it rests on. Nature must have time to renew what we take out, deal with what we release and keep the functions that we and other species depend on.

In TSS this is described with the help of Doughnut Economics. The outer edge of the doughnut is the planetary boundaries, the ecological ceiling for how much pressure we can put on the planet. The inner edge is the social foundation, the basic needs that every person should have met. A sustainable society fits between the foundation and the ceiling.

## Is the ecological more important than the economic and the social?

It is more fundamental, but not more important in itself. The economy and social life depend on nature, but nature does not depend on the economy. That is why the ecological sets the limits for what is possible.

Within those limits there is still great room for choice. How we live, work and distribute resources is decided in the economic, social and democratic systems. A society can stay within planetary boundaries and still be unjust or undemocratic, and then it is not sustainable either.

## How is the ecological connected with the other parts of society?

In every direction. The economy determines how hard nature is pressed through what is produced and how. The social and cultural systems influence what we see as a good life and how much we consume. Democracy decides which rules apply and whether long-term decisions can be made.

In the other direction, the state of nature affects every part of society. Drought and floods hit the economy, increase injustice and can lead to conflict and displacement. Ecological problems therefore quickly become social and political problems.

## What needs to change?

Above all, three things. The pressure needs to fall, so that society's extraction and emissions fit within planetary boundaries. The cycles need to be closed, so that what we leave behind becomes a resource again. And nature needs to be given back space, so that its resilience can recover.

None of this can happen through environmental policy alone. It requires changes in how the economy measures success, what is profitable, how cities and transport are built, how food is produced and what we see as a good life.

## Who decides over nature?

Everyone and no one. Landowners, companies, municipalities, states and international agreements affect nature at different levels. But much of nature, such as the atmosphere, the oceans and biodiversity, is shared and owned by nobody. That makes it easy to overuse. Those who are hit hardest, future generations and other species, also have no voice in the decisions.

There are still examples of shared decisions that work. The Montreal Protocol, which saved the ozone layer, is the clearest. In 2009 the economist Elinor Ostrom received the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel for showing that local communities can often manage shared resources well, if they have clear rules and real influence. The question of who decides over nature is therefore fundamentally a democratic question.

## So what is the answer, really?

A sustainable society lives on nature's interest, not on its capital. It does not take out more than can be renewed, does not release more than nature can deal with and leaves room for nature to recover.

That is not a goal that stands in the way of human welfare. It is a precondition for it. Without working ecosystems there is no economy, and without a stable climate it becomes harder to build secure and just societies.

**So the question is not whether nature should be allowed to set limits, but how we build a good life for all within them.**
