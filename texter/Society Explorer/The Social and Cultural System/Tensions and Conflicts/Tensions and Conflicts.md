## What is the difference between a tension and a conflict?

A tension arises when needs, interests, values or resources are set against each other. It can be about who should get a home, how costs should be shared or which direction society should take. A tension can exist for a long time while people still talk and work together.

A conflict arises when the tension is not dealt with and the oppositions sharpen. If people are not heard, if the distribution is seen as unfair or if groups stop trusting each other, the tension can grow into a conflict. Conflicts do not have to lead to violence, but they become destructive when dialogue and fair processes are missing.

## Should all tensions be removed?

No. A society where nobody disagrees would be neither possible nor desirable. Tensions can make problems visible, challenge old solutions and drive change. Democracy is to a large extent a way of handling tensions without violence.

The aim is therefore not to silence disagreements or force agreement. A robust society is not a society without tensions, but a society that can detect, understand and handle them before they become destructive.

## How can a tension grow into a destructive conflict?

By being reinforced when it is left unaddressed. People start to interpret every event as proof that the other side is a threat. Conversation is replaced by suspicion, groups are set against each other and compromises are seen as betrayal.

One possible course is tension, reinforcement, polarisation, conflict and at worst violence. It is not an automatic chain, and it can be broken at several points. The earlier the disagreement becomes visible and can be talked about, the easier it is to handle.

The image of electrical tension can help: something builds up until it discharges. But societies are not physics. People can interpret, negotiate and choose a different path.

## Can a society tip over, like a lake or a fish stock?

In ways that resemble nature, yes. Researchers speak of **social resilience**: the ability of groups and societies to cope with stress, adapt and, when needed, change their institutions. Long-standing injustices can wear that ability down without it showing. The risk of violence rises especially when divides coincide, so that the same ethnic or religious group is disadvantaged both economically and politically. Most societies with many ethnic groups are nevertheless peaceful. Difference in itself rarely leads to violence.

Once violence has broken out, removing the cause is not enough. Fear and mistrust live on long after the fighting has stopped.

The legacy can also live on long after power has changed hands. In Rwanda, the Belgian colonial power favoured the Tutsi, who were a minority, and required everyone to carry identity cards stating their ethnicity. The country became independent in 1962, but the division lived on. In the genocide of 1994, between half a million and a million Tutsi and moderate Hutu were killed. Colonialism alone does not explain the genocide, which was organised by politicians and militias in Rwanda. But it had created the dividing line they exploited. The research behind the 2024 economics prize also shows that institutions from the colonial era still affect the differences in prosperity between countries.

What holds firm is often the ties between groups. In the Indian city of Calicut, Hindus and Muslims shared trade associations, trade unions and reading clubs, and when tensions rose, peace committees could quickly be formed. In Aligarh, where the groups lived more separately, false reports set off violence. The link holds better for riots than for civil wars, but it shows that cohesion can be built before it is needed.

The comparison has its limits. People can choose, and leaders can deliberately stir up divisions. Nor is resilience always a good thing, because an unjust society can be resilient too. So it is not enough to ask whether society holds. We must also ask who it holds for.

## What kinds of tension are there in society?

Many, and they often overlap. There are economic tensions over incomes and costs, ecological tensions over land, water and natural resources and social tensions over living conditions and belonging. There are tensions about power and ownership, politics, culture and religion, about which information to trust and between generations.

Many of them are built into the very construction of society: country and city, a large or small welfare state, what should be privately owned and what should be shared. None of these tensions is a problem in itself. The problem arises when society lacks ways of dealing with them.

There are also systemic tensions, when the way one system works creates problems that other parts of society have to bear. An economy that depends on economic growth can, for example, create tensions with nature's limits, which eventually become social and political.

## Does the transition create new tensions?

Yes, and it is better to say so openly. Activities that emit a lot will shrink, and those who work there risk their jobs. Higher prices for fossil energy hit harder those who live in the countryside and depend on their car. Wind turbines and mines for the metals of the transition often end up in someone else's local area.

Such tensions cannot be wished away, but they can be handled. That requires those affected to be able to take part and have influence, costs and benefits to be shared fairly and nobody to be left alone with the change.

A shared vision of a sustainable society does not remove disagreement. But it can move it, from whether we should make the transition to how. A shared direction, different paths.

## How do we handle tensions before they become destructive?

By first seeing them and then understanding them. What is set against what? Who is affected, who has influence and who bears the consequences? That requires examining what is actually happening, instead of starting from assumptions about the other side's intentions.

Then forms are needed for weighing interests and making decisions: dialogue, negotiation, independent review, political decisions or public consultations. Different questions need different forms. What matters is that people can make themselves heard and that power and responsibility can be scrutinised.

After the decision, the consequences need to be followed up. Did the tension ease, or did it just move somewhere else? A robust society can correct its course when a solution does not work.

## So what is the answer, really?

Tensions are a normal part of all social life and can drive learning and change. They only become dangerous when they are not visible, when people cannot have influence or when disagreements are allowed to grow into polarisation.

The transition will create new tensions. What matters is whether society can handle them fairly and openly. That ability is one of the most important preconditions for a sustainable society.

**The question is not how we avoid tensions, but how we handle them before they become conflicts.**
