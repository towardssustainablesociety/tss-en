## Why does work mean so much to us?

Because it gives more than a wage. Work can give structure to everyday life, community, identity and the feeling of being needed. For many people, the answer to the question "what do you do?" is a large part of how they see themselves.

But when paid work becomes the measure of a person's worth, other things become invisible. Someone who looks after children or elderly parents, is active in an association or is studying is also contributing, even if it does not show in their pay packet.

## How much of society's work is unpaid?

A lot. The International Labour Organization (ILO) has calculated that people around the world spend around 16 billion hours a day on **unpaid care work**, such as cooking, cleaning and looking after children, the sick and the elderly. About three quarters of it is done by women. Valued at the minimum wage, it is equivalent to around 9 per cent of world GDP.

Unpaid work is not counted in GDP, but without it neither families, workplaces nor society would function. A society that counts only paid work therefore sees only part of what holds it up.

## Does everyone have to contribute in the same way?

No. The ability to contribute varies through life. Some can work full time, others part time or for certain periods. Illness, disability, age and caring responsibilities affect what is possible.

That is why we need to distinguish between not being able to contribute in a particular way, wanting to but lacking the opportunity, and being able to but not wanting to. The situations are different and should not be treated as if they were the same.

## What do we owe each other?

To contribute to the foundation, if we can. A society is built on reciprocity. Food has to be grown, the sick cared for and children taught, and those who are able are expected to contribute to what everyone needs. The contribution can be paid work, but also care, associations or something else that helps people and society.

On the other hand, nobody is obliged to work to produce other people's extra quality of life. Someone who is content with the foundation and contributes to what we share has done their part. That is a different view from the usual one, in which work in itself is seen as a duty regardless of what it produces.

## What happens when making a living requires work that harms society?

Then the individual is in a difficult position. Many jobs are in activities that emit a lot, use a lot of resources or drive unsustainable consumption. Those who work there need the wage, even if the activity contributes to problems that society has to solve.

It is not reasonable to put the responsibility on the individual employee. Nobody should have to choose between making a living and contributing to a sustainable society. It is society's task to make sure there are sustainable jobs and ways to get to them, through investment, education and transition support.

At the same time, society cannot close its eyes to what an activity does just because it creates jobs. Preserving unsustainable jobs is no solution, but neither is closing them down without offering anything else.

## What security is needed when working life changes?

Security that makes it possible to move, not just to stay. The transition will make some jobs redundant and create others. Automation and AI may also reduce the need for human work in many areas.

If security is tied only to your current job, every change becomes a threat. If it is built instead on education, transition support and working welfare, change becomes possible to handle. How work can be shared out when less of it is needed is covered in Work and Livelihoods in the Economic System.

## So what is the answer, really?

Work is important for both the individual and society, but a person's worth cannot be measured in paid work. A large part of what holds society up is unpaid and does not show in the statistics.

A sustainable society is built on reciprocity around the foundation: those who can contribute to what everyone needs, in the way that works for them. The responsibility for there being sustainable work to contribute with lies with society, not with the individual.

**The question is not how many jobs we can create, but which work is needed and how we share it.**
