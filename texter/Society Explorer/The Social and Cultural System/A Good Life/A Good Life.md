## What does a person need to live a good life?

A good life looks different for different people, but the foundation is surprisingly similar. Everyone needs food, water, housing, health and security. Just as important, but easier to forget, are relationships, being able to learn, being able to take part and having something meaningful to do.

The Chilean economist Manfred Max-Neef argued that human **basic needs** are few and the same in all cultures and all times: subsistence, protection, affection, understanding, participation, leisure, creation, identity and freedom. What differs are the ways of meeting them. The need for protection can be met with a cottage or a villa, the need for community with a choir or a football team.

That difference matters. The needs cannot be bargained away, but the ways of meeting them can be chosen.

## What is the difference between basic needs and everything else we want?

Basic needs are what everyone needs in order to live a dignified life. In Doughnut Economics they are together called the social foundation. Below that level, there is deprivation.

Above the foundation lies everything that makes life richer for many: travel, hobbies, experiences, a higher material standard. It is no less human to want such things. But it is something other than a basic need, and society does not have the same responsibility for making sure everyone gets it.

The line is not always sharp, and it moves over time. Fifty years ago, having your own telephone was not a given. Today it is hard to look for work or contact public agencies without a mobile phone. A sustainable society needs to make sure nobody falls below the foundation. How much room there is above it is decided by planetary boundaries.

## Don't we become happier by getting richer?

Yes, usually, but that is not the whole answer. This is one of the strongest arguments for growth, and it should be taken seriously. For someone living in poverty, more money makes an enormous difference. Even in rich countries, people on average feel better the higher their income. A large American study from 2023 found no clear income level at which more money stops making a difference for most people.

But the same study showed that for those who are already unhappy, well-being levels off at a certain income. Money cannot buy away loneliness, illness or a lack of meaning. We also compare ourselves with each other, and what once felt like luxury soon becomes normal.

The question for a sustainable society is therefore not whether money matters. It is whether more consumption is the best way to increase people's well-being once their basic needs are already met.

## Are the planet's resources enough for a good life for all?

Yes, for the basic needs. A study from 2020 calculated that ten billion people could have a decent standard of living in 2050 with about 60 per cent less energy than the world uses today. That includes a sufficiently large and warm home with hot water, a fridge, a washing machine, a computer and a mobile phone in every household, healthcare and schooling for all, and good public transport.

Another study, of over 150 countries, found that no country today meets its people's basic needs in a way that would be sustainable if everyone lived that way. So the problem is not that the foundation is too expensive for the planet. The problem is how much is piled on top of it, and how unevenly it is distributed.

There is therefore room for more than the foundation, but not unlimited room. How that room is shared out is one of the big questions for a sustainable society.

## What security should society offer?

The security of knowing that basic needs will be met, even when life does not go to plan. Someone who falls ill, loses their job or cares for a relative should not fall below the social foundation.

That security is also a precondition for the transition. Nobody should have to choose between making a living and contributing to a sustainable society. It is society's task to make sure there are sustainable jobs and ways to get to them, not the individual's task to decide which jobs are too unsustainable to take.

Security can be created in many ways: through welfare, insurance, close relationships, associations and the local community. Welfare is an important tool, but it is not the same thing as basic needs. It is one of the ways of making sure they are met.

## Is someone a bad citizen if they are content with the foundation?

No. A society is built on reciprocity, but the reciprocity concerns the foundation. Someone has to grow the food, care for the sick and keep the water clean. Those who are able can be expected to contribute to what everyone needs, and that can happen in many ways: through paid work, care, associations or something else.

But nobody is obliged to work to produce other people's extra quality of life. Someone who is content with little and still contributes to what we share is not a bad citizen. From a sustainability point of view, they are rather a role model, since their footprint is smaller.

That does not mean it is wrong to want more. It means that a good life does not have to be measured by how much you consume.

## So what is the answer, really?

A good life requires basic needs to be met: food, housing, health, security, relationships, learning and participation. They are few and shared by everyone. What varies are the ways of meeting them, and that is where both the greatest problems and the greatest opportunities for a sustainable society lie.

Research shows that the planet has enough for the foundation for everyone. What it does not have enough for is a life in which more and more people strive towards an ever higher ceiling. A sustainable society therefore first needs to make sure nobody falls below the foundation, and then share out the room above it in a way the planet can bear.

**The question is not how much we can get, but what we need to live well.**
