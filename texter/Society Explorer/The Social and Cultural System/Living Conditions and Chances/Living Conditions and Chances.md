## Doesn't everyone have the same chances in a country like Sweden?

Formally yes, in practice no. Everyone has the same right to go to school, look for work and vote. But what you can do with your rights depends on where you grow up, what resources your family has, how you are doing and how you are treated.

Someone without a secure home, with poor health or caring for a sick relative finds it harder to get an education or change jobs, even if the right exists on paper. Equal rights are necessary, but they are not enough for equal chances.

## How much does it matter where you grow up?

A great deal, but more in some countries than others. Researchers talk about **social mobility**, meaning how easily children can have a different life from their parents. The OECD has calculated how many generations it takes before the descendants of a family in the lowest-earning ten per cent reach an average income. On average across OECD countries it takes four to five generations. In Denmark it takes two.

The difference shows that social mobility is not a law of nature. It is affected by schools, welfare, housing policy, taxes and the labour market. Societies can make it easier or harder for children to get different chances from their parents.

## Why do differences reinforce themselves?

Because living conditions are connected. Children's upbringing affects health and schooling, which affect work and income, which affect where you can live and which school your own children go to.

A lack of resources also makes it harder to influence decisions. Those who already have the least then risk getting even less influence. That is why the problems can rarely be solved one at a time. Looking only at schools, or only at housing, misses how they reinforce each other.

## Isn't inequality the price of prosperity?

Partly, but the link is weaker than it is often made to sound. The argument for differences is that they create incentives: those who can earn more make more effort, and the cake gets bigger for everyone. There is something in that. A society where everyone gets exactly the same regardless of effort would probably work badly.

But the Nordic countries have long combined comparatively small differences with high prosperity and high social mobility. That suggests large differences are not a precondition for prosperity. Large differences in wealth also risk becoming differences in power, which is described in more detail in Power and Distribution in the Economic System.

## Is fairness treating everyone the same?

Not always. Sometimes fairness requires the same rules to apply to everyone. In other cases people need different support to get the same real chance. A child with dyslexia, a person with a long-term illness and a newly arrived immigrant who has to learn the language do not need the same thing, even though they are all of equal worth.

Since resources are limited, society also has to be able to explain how support is distributed and why. Openness about priorities is part of fairness.

## Can sustainability and good living conditions go together?

Yes, but only if the transition is fair. If climate measures hit hardest those who already have the least, for example through higher fuel prices for people in the countryside who depend on their car, they risk losing support. The yellow vests protests in France in 2018 began as a protest against a higher fuel tax.

At the same time, ecological limits can never be a reason to accept poverty or exclusion. The social foundation and the ecological ceiling belong together, and sustainable policy needs to respect both.

## So what is the answer, really?

People's chances are shaped not only by their own choices but also by the conditions they grow up and live in. Equal rights are the foundation, but real chances also require housing, health, education and support when it is needed.

That Denmark manages in two generations what on average takes four to five shows that it can be influenced. It is also a sustainability question: a transition that increases injustice will not win support.

**The question is not whether everyone has the same rights, but whether everyone has a real chance to use them.**
