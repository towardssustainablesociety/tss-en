## What is the difference between norms, values and ideals?

Norms are often unspoken expectations about how we should behave. Values are what we think is important or right. Ideals are pictures of how something ought to be, for example what a successful life or a successful person looks like.

They are connected. A value can shape an ideal, and an ideal becomes a norm when we start to expect others to live up to it. Norms make everyday life understandable and create community. But they are not neutral: they affect which choices are rewarded and who risks being left out.

## Who decides what counts as a successful life?

No single person, but many have influence. Family, school, workplaces, culture and religion shape our ideals, and so do advertising and social media. When status is linked to income, consumption and job title, it affects what people strive for and how they see themselves and others.

If success is shown mainly as individual achievement and high consumption, other contributions become less visible: care, associations, creativity or work that is important but poorly paid.

## Why do norms survive even when they cause problems?

Because they feel obvious. We learn them early and meet them everywhere. They are kept alive not only by rules, but by approval, criticism, habits and the fear of standing out.

That makes it hard to see the alternatives. If economic growth, competition and ever-increasing productivity are treated as self-evident goals, it becomes hard even to ask what actually contributes to a good life.

## Isn't it up to each of us how we want to live?

Yes, fundamentally. The freedom to shape your own life is one of the most important values we have, and a society that tries to steer people's lifestyles in detail quickly becomes oppressive.

But our choices are not made in a vacuum. Norms, prices, advertising and the way cities are built already steer what is easy and hard, normal and strange. Someone who wants to live without a car in a society built for the car does not have the same freedom as someone who wants to drive. The question is therefore rarely whether our choices should be influenced, but by what, and whether it is something we have chosen together.

## How do norms change?

Often slowly, and then suddenly. Smoking indoors, seat belts and fathers taking parental leave are examples of how something that was once unusual or taken for granted has switched places within a few decades. Norms change when people see their consequences, meet other ways of living and can try new habits.

Research suggests that a minority can overturn an established norm once it becomes large enough. In a much-discussed experiment from 2018, it was enough for about a quarter of a group to stick consistently to a new behaviour for the rest to follow. Below that level, almost nothing happened. The experiment was small and simplified, but it shows why change often looks impossible for a long time and then happens fast.

Change rarely works if people are just told that their way of living is wrong. It works better when the new way is possible, visible and easy to choose.

## What ideals does a sustainable society need?

Ideals that value more than economic success and consumption. Care, trust, participation, fairness and responsibility for what we share also need to count. One such ideal is that a good life is not measured by how much you have, and that someone who is content with little and contributes to what we share is a role model rather than a loser.

That does not mean everyone should have the same ideals. It means that society's norms should make it easier, not harder, to live well within planetary boundaries.

## So what is the answer, really?

Norms, values and ideals shape what we see as normal, important and possible. They create community and direction, but they can also keep unsustainable ways of living in place long after we have understood the consequences.

Norms can be changed, and sometimes it happens quickly once enough people begin. That does not require everyone to think alike. It requires norms to become visible and open to discussion.

**The question is not whether our choices are influenced, but by what, and whether we have chosen it together.**
