## Why is trust so important?

Because almost all cooperation is built on it. When we can trust that others keep agreements, that rules apply equally to everyone and that public agencies do their job, everyday life becomes simpler and cheaper. We do not need to check everything ourselves.

Sweden is one of the countries where trust is highest. In the international World Values Survey in 2022, around 63 per cent of Swedes said that most people can be trusted. In the United States the share was 37 per cent and in Brazil 7 per cent. High trust is an asset that is easy to take for granted, and it is easier to lose than to build.

## What builds trust, and what destroys it?

Trust is built through experience. It grows when people are treated fairly, when rules are applied equally and when it is possible to get things put right when something goes wrong. It is damaged by corruption, arbitrariness and the feeling that some get away with things others are punished for.

Trust does not mean being gullible. A society with high trust needs transparency, scrutiny and the chance to complain. That may sound contradictory, but it is precisely the chance to question that makes people dare to trust the system.

## What makes people feel that they belong?

Being seen, needed and taken seriously. Belonging grows in family and friendship, but also at school, at work, in associations and in the neighbourhood. Being able to contribute strengthens the feeling of being part of something bigger.

Belonging does not require everyone to have the same background or opinions. But it does require that you do not constantly have to prove that you deserve to be included.

## Do we have to agree in order to belong together?

No. A society can hold different beliefs, cultures and views of a good life. Community does not need to rest on everyone thinking alike, but on recognising each other's equal worth and following shared rules of the game.

It becomes harder when disagreement turns into suspicion and other groups start to be seen as a threat. Then people are divided into "us" and "them", and the community that was supposed to carry us becomes something that shuts others out.

## Where is the line between freedom and shared rules?

Where our choices harm others. We want to be able to choose how we live, what we believe and which communities we seek out. But freedom for one group must not mean that others are forced to submit. Tradition, culture or conviction can never justify violence, coercion or denying someone their basic rights.

At the same time, rules can restrict more than necessary. A society that tries to steer people's lives in detail crowds out diversity and personal responsibility. Shared rules therefore need to be justified, open to scrutiny and changeable when they have unreasonable consequences.

## Don't large differences make it harder to trust each other?

Partly, and that should not be denied. A review of a large number of studies from 2020 found a negative link between ethnic diversity and trust. The link was strongest for trust in neighbours and when diversity was measured in the local area.

But trust is also strongly affected by how society works. Fair institutions, low corruption, small economic differences and meeting places where people actually do things together build trust even between people who are different. That is why the question of trust is so closely linked with the question of living conditions.

## So what is the answer, really?

Trust and community are the social glue that makes everything else work. They grow when people are treated fairly, can take part and can trust that rules apply equally to everyone. They do not require everyone to be alike or to agree.

Sweden's high trust is one of the country's greatest assets for the transition. Difficult decisions become easier to make and carry out when people trust each other and the institutions.

**The question is not whether we are alike enough to trust each other, but whether society is fair enough for us to be able to.**
