## Does the Society Map apply at every level?

Yes. The four societal systems exist wherever people live and decide together, from the household to the world. They look different at different levels, but they are always there and they are always connected.

TSS distinguishes eight **levels of society**:

- **The household:** the family or the people you live with. This is where everyday choices about food, housing, travel and time are made.

- **Associations and neighbourhoods:** the housing cooperative, the sports club, the parish, the village association. Here people come together voluntarily and decide together.

- **Organisations:** the workplace, meaning companies, public agencies and other organisations. This is where most of what we need is produced.

- **The municipality:** in Sweden, the 290 municipalities are responsible for schools, care of the elderly, water, waste and land use, among other things.

- **The region:** the 21 Swedish regions are mainly responsible for healthcare and, together with the municipalities, for public transport.

- **The state:** parliament, government and public agencies. This is where laws, taxes and the basic rules of welfare are decided.

- **The EU:** shared rules on trade, competition, agriculture and emissions in 27 countries.

- **The world:** the UN, global agreements and world trade. This is where the questions lie that no country can handle alone.

The levels are not a staircase where the top decides everything. Organisations also cut across them: a multinational company is present at once in a Swedish municipality and on the world market. The same goes for the media and public debate, from the local newspaper to the global platforms, as described in [Knowledge and Public Debate](<../../The Democratic System/Knowledge and Public Debate/Knowledge and Public Debate.md>) in the Democratic System.

## What does the same question look like at different levels?

Take the journey to work. It looks like a private choice, but it is decided at every level at once. The example is Swedish, but the pattern is the same everywhere.

The household chooses car, bus or bike, and where to live in relation to work. The employer decides whether there is free parking, a bike shed or the option of working from home. The municipality decides where homes and workplaces go and whether there are cycle paths. The region and the municipalities decide how often the bus runs. The state sets the tax on petrol and diesel and decides the tax deduction for travel to work. From 2028 the EU is introducing an emissions cap for fuels used in road transport and heating. And the climate agreements between the world's countries set the goal that all this is meant to lead towards.

At every level all four systems are present: the emissions (ecological), the cost (economic), the habits and what counts as normal (social) and who decides (democratic). What differs is how much room for action there is and how far the consequences reach.

The example also shows why things go wrong when one level acts alone. A higher fuel price without a bus to take hits those who live in the countryside, as described in [Living Conditions and Chances](<../../The Social and Cultural System/Living Conditions and Chances/Living Conditions and Chances.md>) in the Social and Cultural System.

## What can the household influence?

More than you might think, but less than is often said. In Sweden, household consumption accounts for around 60 per cent of the emissions caused by Swedes' consumption, in Sweden and in other countries. The rest comes from public consumption, such as schools and hospitals, and from investment in buildings and machinery. What the household eats, how it lives and how it travels therefore really matters.

But the household chooses among what is available, at the prices that apply. That is why the household's greatest influence often goes through people's other roles: as voters, as employees, as members of associations and as role models for neighbours and children.

The household is also a small economy in itself. What is cooked, shared, lent out or done together is not counted in GDP, but it reduces the need to buy new things.

## What can associations and organisations influence?

A lot, and it is often forgotten when the debate is only about the individual or the state. A housing cooperative decides on heating, solar panels and charging points for hundreds of people at once. Associations, cooperatives and jointly owned property associations are also examples of people being able to manage something together without it having to be owned by the state or a company, as described in [Ownership and Basic Security](<../../The Economic System/Ownership and Basic Security/Ownership and Basic Security.md>) in the Economic System.

Organisations decide which materials and energy are used, how work is organised and what suppliers must live up to. Public organisations are also big buyers. In Sweden, public consumption and investment together account for around 40 per cent of the emissions from Swedes' consumption.

But organisations, too, operate within frameworks that others set. A company that chooses a more expensive sustainable solution loses out in competition if the frameworks are not the same for everyone. That is why many organisations have an interest of their own in clear and shared rules.

## What can the municipality and the region influence?

Much of what shapes everyday life. Under the Swedish Planning and Building Act, it is the municipality that decides how land and water are to be used. Where homes, shops, schools and workplaces go decides for a long time to come how far people have to travel and whether it is possible to live without a car.

The municipality is also responsible for water and sewerage, waste, schools and care, and is a big employer and buyer. The region is responsible for healthcare, which accounts for around 90 per cent of Swedish regions' spending, and for public transport together with the municipalities.

Closeness is a strength. Local decisions can be seen, tested and influenced. The Nobel economics laureate Elinor Ostrom argued that the climate question cannot wait for a single global agreement. Many levels acting at once, testing solutions and learning from each other get further. The weakness is that municipalities compete for residents and businesses, and that a municipality cannot tax petrol or set requirements for goods sold across the whole country.

## What is decided in parliament, in the EU and globally?

The frameworks for all the other levels. Parliament decides on laws, taxes and welfare, and on what municipalities and regions may and must do. This is where the prices and rules are set that decide what is easy and what is hard for households and organisations. That is described in [Production and Consumption](<../../The Economic System/Production and Consumption/Production and Consumption.md>) in the Economic System.

The EU sets shared rules for trade and competition and for much of environmental policy. Emissions trading is an example: a cap that applies to all member states, so that no country can compete by having lower requirements.

Some questions can only be solved globally, because the climate, the oceans and the ozone layer do not care about borders. Almost all the world's countries have joined the Paris Agreement from 2015. But there is no world government. Agreements only work if countries carry them out themselves, and then the path leads back down through all the levels.

## Isn't it still only politicians who can change anything?

No, but nothing works without politics. The objection has a point. The biggest frameworks, such as taxes, laws and international agreements, are decided by politicians, and nobody else can replace them. A household cannot introduce an emissions cap.

But politics and the other levels are connected in both directions. Politicians make the decisions they think they can win support for. What has first been tried in a municipality, an association or a company shows that something works and makes it easier to decide on it for everyone. Popular movements have driven much of what is taken for granted today, and norms can turn once enough people have begun, as described in [Norms, Values and Ideals](<../../The Social and Cultural System/Norms, Values and Ideals/Norms, Values and Ideals.md>) in the Social and Cultural System.

Those who only wait for the politicians are therefore waiting for something that is largely built from below.

## So what is the answer, really?

The Society Map applies at every level, from the household to the world. At every level the four systems are present, and at every level there is room for action of its own. No level is enough on its own.

The global level sets the goal. The EU and the state set the frameworks. The municipality and the region build what makes the sustainable choice possible. Organisations produce within the frameworks, and households and associations live, test and show the way. At the same time, it is the same people who vote the frameworks into being.

**The question is not where change should begin, but how decisions at every level can support each other.**
